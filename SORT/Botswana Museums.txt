*Botswana National Museum* 🇧🇼  
_Full name: Botswana National Museum & Art Gallery_  
_Location: Gaborone, next to the Government Enclave_

For people interested in culture and history, this is the #1 place to start in Botswana. It’s not big, but it tells the whole 2-million-year story of the country in one building.

*1. HISTORY + PURPOSE*
*Opened*: 1968, just 2 years after Botswana independence.  
*Why it was built*: The new government wanted one place to collect, protect, and show Batswana heritage. During colonial times many artifacts were taken to South Africa and the UK. This museum brought them home.  
*Role*: It’s the national archive for history, art, archaeology, and natural history. It also runs smaller museums around the country like Phuthadikobo in Mochudi and Supa Ngwao in Francistown.

*2. WHAT’S INSIDE - THE MAIN GALLERIES*
You can do the whole museum in 1-2 hours, but every room is packed.

*A. NATURAL HISTORY*
- *Dinosaurs + Fossils*: Bones from the Karoo period. Botswana had dinosaurs 200 million years ago.
- *Stone Age tools*: Hand axes 1 million years old from the Kalahari. This shows people have lived here longer than anywhere.
- *Animals*: Full taxidermy of Botswana wildlife - elephant, lion, giraffe, and rare species. Used to teach kids about the ecosystem.

*B. ARCHAEOLOGY + EARLY HISTORY*
- *Tsodilo Hills artifacts*: Tools and replicas from the rock art site
- *Iron Age*: Pottery, iron smelting tools, and beads from 1000-1800 AD. Shows trade with the coast
- *San artifacts*: Hunting tools, ostrich-shell beads, and snare traps

*C. CULTURE + ETHNOGRAPHY*
This is what most people come for.
- *Traditional crafts*: Real examples of *monolayer baskets* from Etsha, *woodcarvings* from Ngamiland, *pottery* from Thamaga
- *Clothing*: `leteisi` dresses, `megopo` skirts, beadwork, and chief’s regalia
- *Music*: Drums, `segaba` 1-string fiddle, and recordings of Setapa and Phathisi
- *Home life*: A recreated traditional hut `ntlo` with `lekgoa` and cooking pots

*D. ART GALLERY*
Upstairs. Shows contemporary Botswana artists + traveling exhibitions. 
Artists like *Thomas Nkhwa* and *Meekness Serema* are featured. It’s how the museum connects old culture to new artists.

*E. HISTORY WING*
- *Independence*: Photos and documents from 1966 when Botswana became independent
- *Diamonds + Democracy*: How diamonds funded schools and roads
- *Presidents*: Photos of all Botswana presidents

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians and culture people, the museum matters for 3 reasons:

1.  *It saved the story*: Without it, many baskets, stools, and tools would be lost. Elders from villages still come to donate family items.
2.  *It teaches Botho*: The displays aren’t just "old things". Labels explain _why_ a 3-legged stool meant authority, or _why_ baskets had patterns. It’s about values.
3.  *It’s for Batswana first*: School kids visit every year. It’s free for citizens. The goal was never just tourists — it was to make sure Batswana know their own history.

During the 1980s, the museum helped record oral histories from elders before they passed away. Those recordings are still in the archive.

*4. TODAY*
*Location*: Central Gaborone, across from the National Library. Easy to walk to from the mall.  
*Cost*: Very cheap. Citizens P5, Visitors P20.  
*What else*: There’s a garden with traditional plants, a restaurant, and a gift shop selling authentic baskets and crafts — not tourist junk.  
*Research*: Students and researchers use the library and archives upstairs.

*Special events*: The museum runs Culture Day, International Museum Day, and hosts dance groups for Phathisi and Setapa performances in the courtyard.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"The Botswana National Museum in Gaborone is the country’s memory box. In one building you go from 1-million-year-old stone tools, to San rock art, to Iron Age pottery, to the baskets and carvings that people still make today, to independence in 1966. It was built right after independence so Batswana could own their history. You’ll see how people survived the Kalahari with mongongo nuts and phane, how the Delta people carved mokoros, and how art and craft became identity."

*Quick facts:*
- *Best for*: First-time visitors who want "Botswana in 2 hours"
- *Don’t miss*: The basket gallery and the recreated traditional village display
- *Time needed*: 1.5 hours

*Comparison:*
- *South Africa*: Big museums in Joburg/Cape Town
- *Zimbabwe*: Great Zimbabwe museum  
- *Botswana*: Small, but focused on telling one national story clearly

*Domboshaba Ruins* 🇧🇼  
_Also spelled: Dhomboshaba. Means "Red Rock" in Kalanga_  
_Location: Near Masunga, North-East District, ∼70km from Francistown_

For people interested in culture and history, *Domboshaba is Botswana’s "Great Zimbabwe"*. It’s the most important stone ruin in the country and shows that Botswana had big kingdoms long before colonialism.

*1. WHAT IT IS + WHO BUILT IT*
*The site*: A hilltop stone-walled settlement from the *Iron Age, 1450 - 1650 AD*.  
*Who built it*: The *Bakalanga* people, ancestors of today’s Kalanga in North-East Botswana and western Zimbabwe.  
*Why here*: The hill had good defense, rain, and was on old trade routes to the Indian Ocean coast. 

It’s built in the same style as *Great Zimbabwe and Mapungubwe* — dry stone walls, no mortar, with passages, enclosures, and a main hill fort.

*Key features*:
- *Hill complex*: The chief’s area on top with the best views
- *Valley complex*: Where commoners lived below
- *Stone walls*: Up to 2 meters high in places, with curved walls typical of Kalanga architecture
- *Caves*: Used for storage and rituals

*2. THE HISTORY: WHAT IT TELLS US*
Domboshaba proves 3 big things for Botswana history:

1.  *Kingdoms existed*: Around 1500 AD this was the capital of a Kalanga chiefdom. Population maybe 1000-2000 people. There was cattle, farming, iron smelting, and trade.
2.  *Trade*: Archaeologists found *Chinese porcelain, glass beads, and cloth* here. That means people at Domboshaba were trading all the way to the coast, swapping ivory and gold for imports.
3.  *Continuity*: The Kalanga people still live around Masunga today. Their language, basketry, and clan names connect directly to the people who built Domboshaba. It’s not "lost" — it’s their grandparents’ home.

The site was abandoned around 1650, likely because of drought, wars, and trade routes shifting.

*3. IS THERE A MUSEUM? YES*
*Domboshaba Cultural Museum* is right at the site.

*What’s in the museum:*
1.  *Archaeology room*: Pottery, iron tools, beads, and Chinese porcelain found at the ruins. You can see the actual trade goods.
2.  *Kalanga Culture room*: Displays of *traditional baskets `tetele`*, grinding stones, spears, clothing, and photos of Kalanga ceremonies
3.  *History panels*: Explains the timeline of the site and how it connects to Great Zimbabwe
4.  *Audio-visual*: Videos of Kalanga dances and elders telling oral history

*Outside*: Guided tours walk you up the hill through the actual ruins. The guides are usually local Kalanga people who know the oral stories.

The museum opened in the 1990s and is run by the *National Museum of Botswana*.

*4. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY TODAY*
For Kalanga people and for Botswana history:

1.  *Pride + Identity*: For a long time history books said "nothing big happened in Botswana". Domboshaba proves that’s wrong. Kalanga schools bring kids here to learn their history.
2.  *Living culture*: Every year there’s the *Domboshaba Cultural Festival* in September. Kalanga dance, music, food, and craft. Chiefs come to perform rituals at the site.
3.  *National Monument*: Protected by law. You can’t dig or take stones. It’s a symbol of pre-colonial African engineering.

Elders say the name _Domboshaba_ = "Red Rock" because the granite turns red at sunset.

*5. VISITING TODAY*
*Location*: 15km from Masunga village, on a tarred road from Francistown  
*Hours*: Open daily. Small entrance fee.  
*Guides*: You must have a guide to go up the hill — they explain which wall was for cattle, which was for the chief, etc.  
*Best time*: April - September, cool and dry

There’s also craft sellers at the gate selling Kalanga baskets and woodcarvings.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Domboshaba is Botswana’s 500-year-old stone city. Built by Kalanga kings between 1450-1650, it has dry-stone walls, trade goods from China, and a hilltop palace. It shows that Botswana was part of the big Southern African kingdoms like Great Zimbabwe. Today there’s a museum on site with the actual artifacts, and local Kalanga guides tell both the archaeology and the oral stories. It’s proof that Botswana’s history didn’t start in 1966."

*Quick facts:*
- *Age*: ∼1450-1650 AD
- *Size*: About 10 hectares
- *Similar sites*: Great Zimbabwe, Khami, Mapungubwe
- *Festival*: Domboshaba Cultural Festival every September

*Comparison:*
- *Great Zimbabwe*: Bigger, but in Zimbabwe  
- *Domboshaba*: Smaller, but in Botswana and still connected to living Kalanga communities

*Francistown Museum* 🇧🇼  
_Full name: Supa Ngwao Museum_  
_Location: Francistown, CBD - in the Old Town Hall building_

For people interested in culture and history, *Supa Ngwao* is the best place to understand northern Botswana. "Supa Ngwao" means "Show Culture" in Kalanga. It’s run by the Botswana National Museum.

*1. HISTORY + THE BUILDING*
*Opened as a museum*: 1986  
*The building*: The *Old Town Hall, built 1902*. That alone is history. It’s one of the oldest buildings in Botswana.  
*Why here*: Francistown is Botswana’s 2nd city and the gateway to the north. It was a mining town, a trade hub, and the heartland of *Kalanga, Ndebele, and Bakalanga* people. The museum was put here to collect that story.

During the 1890s-1930s this building was the court, council office, and meeting hall. Gold miners, traders, and chiefs all passed through here.

*2. WHAT’S INSIDE - THE MAIN EXHIBITS*
It’s 3 floors and you can do it in 1 hour, but it’s packed with northern Botswana history.

*A. KALANGA CULTURE GALLERY*
This is the heart of the museum.
- *Basketry*: Huge collection of *Kalanga/Ndebele baskets* `tetele` with geometric patterns. Different from the Okavango "monolayer" style.
- *Pottery*: Traditional pots for beer, water, and cooking. Shows Kalanga designs.
- *Tools*: Grinding stones, hoes, iron spears, and snuff boxes
- *Clothing + Beadwork*: `Ndebele` beadwork with bright colors and patterns that show age and marital status

*B. MINING + FRANCISTOWN HISTORY*
Francistown started because of gold in 1860s. 
- *Gold rush artifacts*: Picks, pans, mine lamps, and photos of the 1890s gold rush
- *Railway*: Francistown was the end of the railway line in 1897. Photos and tickets from that time
- *Old town photos*: How Francistown looked in 1900s vs today

*C. ETHNOGRAPHY OF NORTHERN BOTSWANA*
- *Ndebele history*: Migration stories, shields, and war artifacts
- *San artifacts*: Tools and beads from people who lived around Francistown
- *Music*: `Mbira`, drums, and recordings of Kalanga `hosanna` music

*D. NATURAL HISTORY*
Smaller than Gaborone, but has:
- *Taxidermy*: Animals from the north - elephant, leopard, antelope
- *Geology*: Rocks and minerals from the Francistown gold belt

There’s also a *temporary art gallery* upstairs for local artists.

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians, Supa Ngwao matters for 3 reasons:

1.  *It centers Kalanga history*: National history often focuses on Tswana tribes. This museum makes sure Kalanga, Ndebele, and northern stories aren’t forgotten. Domboshaba artifacts are explained here too.
2.  *Mining + Migration story*: Francistown was built by miners, traders, and migrants from Zimbabwe, Zambia, and SA. The museum shows how that mixed city was formed.
3.  *Living museum*: They don’t just display. They run basket-weaving classes, dance groups, and the *Domboshaba Festival* is organized from here. Elders come to record oral history.

The building itself is a monument — you’re literally learning history inside a 1902 courtroom.

*4. TODAY*
*Location*: Corner of Blue Jacket and Selous Ave, Francistown CBD. You can’t miss the red brick building.  
*Cost*: Citizens P5, Visitors P20. Very cheap.  
*Time*: 1-1.5 hours  
*Guides*: Staff will walk you through and explain Kalanga patterns and mining history.  
*Gift shop*: Sells authentic Kalanga baskets, carvings, and books you won’t find in Gaborone.

*Special events*: Heritage Day, International Museum Day, and school tours are big here.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Supa Ngwao Museum in Francistown is 'Show Culture' in Kalanga. It’s in a 1902 town hall and tells the story of northern Botswana. You’ll see Kalanga baskets and beadwork, gold rush tools from the 1890s, and exhibits on how Francistown became a mining and trade city. It connects Domboshaba ruins to the people living in Masunga today. If Gaborone museum is 'Botswana’s whole story', Francistown museum is 'Northern Botswana’s story'."

*Quick facts:*
- *Best for*: Understanding Kalanga culture and gold rush history
- *Don’t miss*: The basket gallery and the old courtroom photos
- *Nearby*: 70km from Domboshaba Ruins. People often do both in one trip

*Comparison:*
- *Gaborone National Museum*: National overview
- *Phuthadikobo Museum Mochudi*: Bakgatla history
- *Supa Ngwao Francistown*: Kalanga + mining + northern tribes

*Kgosi Bathoen II Museum* 🇧🇼  
_Location: Segopotso Ward, Kanye, Southern District_  
_Also called: Bathoen II Museum / Bangwaketse Museum_

For people interested in culture and history, this museum is about *chiefs, resistance, and how one man changed Botswana*. It’s named after *Kgosi Bathoen II* of the *Bangwaketse* tribe.

*1. WHO WAS KGOSI BATHOEN II?*
*Reign*: 1949 - 1969. He was chief during the most important time in Botswana history.  
*Why he’s famous*:
1.  *Anti-colonial leader*: He fought against British laws and forced labor. He testified at the UN in New York in 1959 demanding independence.
2.  *Father of democracy*: He gave up his chieftainship in 1969 to become a politician. He was the first to say "a chief should not also be a party politician" so he stepped down.
3.  *Education champion*: He pushed Bangwaketse to build schools. Kanye became one of the most educated villages in Botswana because of him.
4.  *Cultural guardian*: He recorded oral history, laws, and customs before they were lost.

He’s buried in Kanye, and his house was turned into a museum.

*2. THE MUSEUM: WHAT IT IS*
*Location*: Segopotso Ward, Kanye. It’s Kgosi Bathoen II’s actual home/office.  
*Opened*: 1994 by the National Museum of Botswana  
*Building*: A traditional + colonial style house with his office, bedroom, and meeting room intact.

*WHAT’S INSIDE*

*A. KGOSI’S OFFICE*
- His *original desk, chair, telephone, and typewriter* 
- Letters he wrote to the British, to the UN, and to other chiefs
- His *chief’s regalia*: `nkwe` leopard skin, walking stick, and beadwork

*B. HISTORY GALLERY*
- *Bangwaketse history*: From the 1700s to today. Photos of all Kgosi of Bangwaketse
- *Struggle for independence*: Documents and photos of Bathoen II at the UN in 1959
- *Kanye story*: How Kanye became the biggest village in Botswana in the 1950s-60s

*C. CULTURE GALLERY*
- *Bangwaketse crafts*: Baskets, pottery, and `diphoko` grinding stones
- *Traditional court `kgotla` items*: The stick used for judgment, record books
- *Music*: Recordings of `setapa` and `phathisi` from Kanye

*D. PERSONAL ITEMS*
His suits, hats, books, and family photos. It feels like he just stepped out. There’s even his Bible.

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians and culture people, this museum shows 3 big things:

1.  *The power of chiefs*: Before 1966, chiefs were the government. Bathoen II used the `kgotla` to mobilize people for schools, clinics, and independence.
2.  *Transition to modern Botswana*: He’s the bridge. A traditional chief who became a modern politician and then retired peacefully. That’s rare in Africa.
3.  *Bangwaketse identity*: The Bangwaketse are the 2nd largest tribe. This museum keeps their songs, praise poems `dithoko`, and history alive. School kids from Kanye come here every year.

Oral history says Bathoen II would sit under the tree in Segopotso and anyone could come talk to him. That tree is still there.

*4. TODAY*
*Location*: Segopotso Ward, Kanye. About 90km from Gaborone on the A1.  
*Run by*: National Museum of Botswana + Bangwaketse Tribal Administration  
*Cost*: Citizens P5, Visitors P20  
*Time needed*: 45 min - 1 hour  
*Guides*: Often a member of the Bangwaketse royal family or elders will show you around and tell stories.

There’s also *Kgosi Bathoen II’s grave* nearby at the royal cemetery. People still go pay respects.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"The Kgosi Bathoen II Museum in Segopotso, Kanye is in the actual home of the chief who led Bangwaketse to independence. From 1949-1969 he fought the British, spoke at the UN, then gave up being chief to help build democracy. The museum has his desk, his letters, and his leopard skin. It’s not just about one man — it’s about how traditional leadership helped create modern Botswana. For Bangwaketse people, this is where their history lives."

*Quick facts:*
- *Don’t miss*: His UN speech notes and the original `kgotla` chair
- *Best time*: Go during `Dikgafela` harvest ceremony in Kanye in August
- *Nearby*: Kanye `kgotla` and the big Baobab tree in the village

*Comparison:*
- *Gaborone National Museum*: Whole country story
- *Phuthadikobo Mochudi*: Bakgatla chiefs
- *Bathoen II Museum Kanye*: One chief who changed a nation

*Khama III Memorial Museum* 🇧🇼  
_Location: Serowe, Central District_  
_Also called: Khama Museum_

For people interested in culture and history, *Khama III Museum is the most important "royal" museum in Botswana*. It’s about the *Bangwato* kings who built modern Botswana. This is where you go to understand where presidents, democracy, and the Khama family come from.

*1. WHO WAS KGOSI KHAMA III?*
*Reign*: 1875 - 1923. 48 years - one of the longest in Botswana history.  
*Nicknames*: "Khama the Good", "Father of the Bangwato"

*Why he’s huge in history:*
1.  *Christian + Modernizer*: He converted to Christianity in 1860s, banned alcohol and witchcraft, and built schools and churches. He made Serowe the biggest village in Africa at the time.
2.  *Protector*: He traveled to London in 1895 with Bathoen I and Sebele I to beg Queen Victoria to stop the British South Africa Company from taking Bechuanaland. He won. That’s why Botswana didn’t become part of Rhodesia.
3.  *Father of a dynasty*: His grandson was *Sir Seretse Khama* - Botswana’s 1st President. His great-grandson is *Lt. Gen. Ian Khama* - 4th President. 3 generations of presidents come from this house.
4.  *Lawmaker*: He wrote down Bangwato laws and used the `kgotla` to govern fairly. "Molao wa ga Khama" - Khama’s law - is still quoted.

He’s buried in Serowe next to the museum.

*2. THE MUSEUM: WHAT IT IS*
*Location*: Serowe, next to the *Old Khama III Kgotla* and the royal cemetery.  
*Opened*: 1994  
*Building*: A mix of his original house, the old school, and new museum wings. You walk through the actual royal compound.

*WHAT’S INSIDE*

*A. KGOSI KHAMA III GALLERY*
- *Personal items*: His Bible, walking stick, glasses, hat, and `kaross`
- *Letters*: Letters to Queen Victoria, to missionaries, and to other chiefs
- *Photos*: Khama III with his wives, with missionaries, and on the 1895 trip to London
- *The London Trip display*: The suit they wore to meet the Queen. This is the most famous exhibit.

*B. BANGWATO ROYAL HISTORY*
- *Family tree*: From Kgosi Sekgoma I → Khama III → Sekgoma II → Tshekedi → Seretse Khama → Ian Khama
- *Royal regalia*: Leopard skins, spears, fly whisks `lefoko` used by chiefs
- *Kgotla items*: The drum used to call people, and record books

*C. SEROWE + MISSION HISTORY*
- *Lond mission*: Photos of the first school and church built in 1904
- *Serowe history*: How it grew to 40,000 people by 1920 with no running water
- *Cattle + Economy*: How Bangwato wealth was built on cattle

*D. OUTSIDE*
- *Khama III’s grave*: In the royal cemetery. Still visited and decorated
- *Old Kgotla tree*: Where Khama held court
- *Khama III Memorial Church*: Built by him in 1923

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians, Khama III Museum shows 4 big things:

1.  *How Botswana survived*: Without the 1895 London trip, Botswana would likely be part of Zimbabwe or South Africa today. Khama III saved the country’s borders.
2.  *Church + State*: He proved a chief could be Christian AND traditional. He used the Bible and the `kgotla` together.
3.  *The Khama dynasty*: Every Botswana president until 2018 came from this family. Understanding Khama III = understanding modern Botswana politics.
4.  *Bangwato identity*: Bangwato are the largest tribe. This museum keeps their praise poems `dithoko`, history, and values alive. "Puso ke ya batho" - "Government is for the people" was Khama’s saying.

Elders in Serowe still say: _"Fa o batla go itse Botswana, ya kwa Serowe"_ - "If you want to know Botswana, go to Serowe".

*4. TODAY*
*Location*: Serowe, Central District. 300km north of Gaborone on the A1.  
*Run by*: National Museum of Botswana + Bangwato Tribal Administration  
*Cost*: Citizens P5, Visitors P20  
*Time needed*: 1.5 - 2 hours  
*Guides*: Often a member of the royal family or Bangwato elder. They tell stories you won’t find in books.

*Special events*: *Khama Day* every July. Speeches, dance, and people visit the grave.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"The Khama III Museum in Serowe is in the home of the chief who saved Botswana. In 1895 Kgosi Khama III went to London to stop Rhodes from taking Bechuanaland. He won. He also built schools, banned alcohol, and created the laws that became Botswana’s democracy. His grandson became the first president. The museum has his Bible, his suit from London, and his grave. If you want to understand where Botswana came from, you start here."

*Quick facts:*
- *Don’t miss*: The 1895 London suit and the family tree to Sir Seretse Khama
- *Nearby*: Khama Rhino Sanctuary - named after him, 25km from Serowe
- *Best time*: July for Khama Day, or any weekday when the kgotla is active

*Comparison of Botswana Chief Museums:*
- *Khama III Museum Serowe*: Saved the country, father of presidents
- *Bathoen II Museum Kanye*: Fought for independence at the UN
- *Sechele I Museum Molepolole*: Fought Boers, met Livingstone
- *Supa Ngwao Francistown*: Kalanga + mining history

*Khama Rhino Sanctuary Education Centre* 🇧🇼  
_Location: 25km outside Serowe, Central District_  
_Full name: Khama Rhino Sanctuary Trust - Education & Interpretation Centre_

For people interested in culture and history, this isn’t just about rhinos. It’s about *how Botswana turned conservation into community, culture, and pride*. It’s named after *Kgosi Khama III* and run by the people of Serowe.

*1. WHY IT WAS CREATED: HISTORY + PURPOSE*
*Opened*: 1992  
*Why here*: By the 1980s rhinos were almost extinct in Botswana due to poaching. The people of Serowe and the Bangwato tribe said "not in our land". They set aside 8585 hectares of bush to protect rhinos.  
*Named for*: *Kgosi Khama III* - the chief who protected his people. Now his name protects rhinos.

*Main purpose - 3 things:*
1.  *Save rhinos*: It’s a breeding sanctuary for *white and black rhino*. One of the few places in Southern Africa where both live together.
2.  *Community project*: Owned and run by a Trust made up of Serowe villagers. Money from tourism goes to schools, clinics, and jobs in Serowe.
3.  *Teach people*: The *Education Centre* teaches Batswana kids why wildlife and culture must be protected together.

Botswana’s idea: _Conservation only works if local people benefit._

*2. WHAT’S AT THE EDUCATION CENTRE*
It’s built at the main gate, before you go on game drive.

*A. INTERPRETATION HALL*
- *Rhino biology*: Skulls, footprints, horns, and info on white vs black rhino
- *Poaching history*: Photos and stories of how rhinos were wiped out in the 70s-80s, and how Botswana fought back
- *Anti-poaching*: Displays of ranger equipment, snares, and how tracking works
- *"Khama III" connection*: Panel explaining why it’s named after the chief who protected his people

*B. CULTURE + COMMUNITY GALLERY*
This is what makes it different from other wildlife centres.
- *Bangwato history*: How Serowe people have always lived with wildlife. Traditional hunting vs conservation
- *Traditional medicine*: Plants used by healers, and why rhino horn is NOT medicine - busting myths
- *School art*: Paintings and projects from Serowe schools about rhinos and the environment

*C. OUTSIDE*
- *Botanical trail*: Labeled trees and plants used for food, medicine, and crafts by Bangwato
- *Viewpoint*: Overlooks a waterhole where rhinos often come
- *Craft shop*: Sells baskets, carvings, and t-shirts made by women from Serowe. 100% of profit goes to them

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians and culture people, the Education Centre teaches 3 big lessons:

1.  *Botho and stewardship*: The Bangwato saying _"Lefatshe le la rona"_ - "The land is ours". That means we must look after it. Khama III made laws to protect cattle and water. This centre continues that.
2.  *Community conservation model*: Unlike private game farms, this is owned by the community. Rangers are from Serowe. Guides are from Serowe. When tourists come, the village benefits. This model is now copied across Botswana.
3.  *Linking past and future*: Khama III protected people from outsiders in 1895. Today the sanctuary protects rhinos from poachers. Same idea: protection.

Every year *3000+ school kids* from Central District come here for free. They learn about rhinos, but also about Bangwato history and responsibility.

*4. WILDLIFE + VISITING*
*Animals*: 35+ rhinos, plus zebra, giraffe, eland, ostrich, and 230 bird species. No predators, so it’s safe for walking.  
*Activities*: Game drives, guided walks, night drives, and rhino tracking on foot with rangers.  
*Accommodation*: Campsites and chalets inside the sanctuary.

*Education programs*:
- *School trips*: Kids learn rhino ID, track animals, and do drama about poaching
- *Teacher training*: Teachers learn how to teach conservation
- *Community talks*: Rangers go to villages to talk about human-wildlife conflict

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"The Khama Rhino Sanctuary Education Centre near Serowe is named after Kgosi Khama III, the chief who saved Botswana. In 1992 the people of Serowe decided to save rhinos the same way - by protecting them as a community. The centre teaches kids that conservation is culture. You learn about rhinos, but also about Bangwato laws, traditional plants, and why the land matters. All the money stays in Serowe for schools and clinics. It’s proof that culture and wildlife can survive together."

*Quick facts:*
- *Size*: 8585 hectares - about the size of Gaborone
- *Rhinos*: Home to both black and white rhino
- *Unique*: You can walk with armed rangers and track rhinos on foot
- *Owned by*: Khama Rhino Sanctuary Trust - 14 villages around Serowe

*Comparison:*
- *Mokolodi Nature Reserve Gaborone*: Education for southern Botswana
- *Khama Rhino Serowe*: Education + community ownership + Bangwato history

*Ntsweng Heritage Museum* 🇧🇼  
_Location: Ntsweng Village, 15km west of Gaborone, South-East District_  
_Also called: Ntsweng Hill / "Old Gaborone"_

For people interested in culture and history, *Ntsweng is where modern Gaborone began*. It’s not a building with glass cases. It’s an *open-air heritage site + small museum* that tells the story of *Kgosi Gaborone* and the founding of the Bakwena capital.

*1. WHO WAS KGOSI GABORONE & WHAT IS NTSWENG?*
*Time*: 1880s - 1890s  
*Who*: *Kgosi Gaborone* of the *Bakwena* tribe. Gaborone city is named after him.  
*What was Ntsweng*: This hill was the capital of the Bakwena from about 1880 to 1890. It had over 10,000 people.

*Why they left*: In 1890 there was a drought and fighting. Kgosi Gaborone moved the people 15km east to where Gaborone city is today, next to the Notwane River. That became "Gaberon" and later "Gaborone".

So Ntsweng = *"Old Gaborone"*. The city you’re in now started here.

*2. THE HERITAGE SITE + MUSEUM*
*Opened as heritage site*: 1990s  
*Run by*: National Museum of Botswana + Bakwena Tribal Administration  

It’s 2 parts:

*A. THE OPEN-AIR SITE ON THE HILL*
You walk the actual village. Nothing is reconstructed, so you see real history.
- *Stone wall foundations*: Remains of 200+ huts `ntlo` in circles. You can see the family compounds
- *Cattle kraals `losaka`*: Huge stone circles where Bakwena kept thousands of cattle. Cattle = wealth
- *Grain pits*: Underground storage pits for sorghum `mabele`
- *Kgotla area*: The flat area where Kgosi Gaborone held court

Guides will show you how the village was laid out: Chief’s area on top, then elders, then everyone else.

*B. THE SMALL INTERPRETATION CENTRE / MUSEUM*
At the base of the hill.
- *Photos*: 1890s photos of Ntsweng taken by missionaries
- *Artifacts found here*: Pottery, grinding stones `diphoko`, iron tools, and beads
- *Bakwena history board*: Family tree: Kgosi Gaborone → Kgosi Sebele I → Kgosi Bathoen → Sir Seretse Khama
- *Maps*: Shows how people moved from Ntsweng → Gaborone Village → Gaborone City in 1966

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians, Ntsweng matters for 3 big reasons:

1.  *The origin of Gaborone*: Every time you say "Gaborone" you’re saying Kgosi Gaborone’s name. This is where his village was. It connects the capital city to a person and a hill.
2.  *Typical 1800s Tswana town*: Archaeologists study Ntsweng to understand how big Tswana capitals worked before cars and shops. The stone walls, cattle by number, and kgotla layout are all still used today.
3.  *Resilience + Movement*: Batswana moved capitals often for water, grass, and safety. Ntsweng → Gaborone is one example. The museum explains this "moving capital" culture.

Elders say: _"Fa o sa itse kwa o tswang, o ka se itse kwa o yang"_ - "If you don’t know where you come from, you won’t know where you’re going."

*4. VISITING TODAY*
*Location*: 15km from Gaborone CBD, off the A1 to Lobatse. Turn at Kgale.  
*Cost*: Citizens P5, Visitors P20  
*Time needed*: 1.5 - 2 hours. Wear walking shoes - it’s a hill climb.  
*Guides*: Bakwena guides from nearby villages. They’ll tell you oral history about Kgosi Gaborone.

*Best time*: May - September. It’s hot and open, no shade.  
*What to bring*: Water, hat. There are no shops on the hill.

There are school trips here every year. Kids learn that their capital has roots.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Ntsweng Heritage Museum is 'Old Gaborone'. In the 1880s this hill was the capital of Kgosi Gaborone and the Bakwena. There were 10,000 people living in stone-walled huts here. In 1890 they moved down to the river and that became Gaborone city. The site has the real foundations of huts and cattle kraals. The small museum shows photos and artifacts. If you want to understand where Gaborone came from, you have to start at Ntsweng."

*Quick facts:*
- *Don’t miss*: Stand on top of the hill and look toward Gaborone city. You’ll see why they moved - better water
- *Connection*: Kgosi Gaborone → Sir Seretse Khama. Same royal line
- *Nearby*: 20min from Gaborone. Easy half-day trip

*Comparison:*
- *National Museum Gaborone*: National story
- *Kgosi Sechele Museum Molepolole*: Bakwena history + Livingstone

*Phuthadikobo Museum* 🇧🇼  
_Location: Mochudi, Kgatleng District_  
_“Phuthadikobo” = "Gather the dust/ashes of the tribe" in Setswana_

For people interested in culture and history, *Phuthadikobo is the home of Bakgatla history*. It’s one of Botswana’s oldest community museums and it tells the story of *Kgosi Kgafela* and the Bakgatla-ba-Kgafela tribe.

*1. HISTORY + THE BUILDING*
*Opened*: 1976  
*Location*: Mochudi, inside the *Old Bakgatla Kgotla* complex. 40km northeast of Gaborone.  
*Run by*: Bakgatla-ba-Kgafela Tribal Administration + National Museum of Botswana  
*Name meaning*: _Phuthadikobo_ = "Gather the ashes". After wars and migration, the chief told people to "gather the ashes" and rebuild the tribe. That’s the museum’s job too - gather and keep Bakgatla history.

*The building*: It’s the old *police cells and court rooms* from the 1930s. So you’re literally in history while learning history.

*2. WHAT’S INSIDE - THE MAIN EXHIBITS*

*A. BAKGATLA ROYAL HISTORY*
This is the heart of the museum.
- *Kgosi Kgafela I & II*: Photos, regalia, and stories. Kgafela II fought the British in 1910 and took part of the tribe to South Africa. That’s why there are Bakgatla in both Botswana and SA today.
- *Royal regalia*: Leopard skins `nkwe`, spears, fly whisks `lefoko`, and the chief’s chair
- *Family tree*: From early Bakgatla chiefs to the current Kgosi Kgafela III

*B. BAKGATLA CULTURE GALLERY*
- *Initiation `bojale + bogwera`*: Photos and explanations of traditional coming-of-age schools. Songs, dress, and teachings
- *Traditional dress*: `mokgabo` skirts, beadwork, and leatherwork unique to Bakgatla
- *Music + Dance*: `setapa` dance costumes, drums, and `segaba` fiddle
- *Houses*: Model of a traditional Bakgatla homestead with `ntlo`, `lelapa`, and grain bins

*C. DAILY LIFE + CRAFTS*
- *Pottery*: Bakgatla women are famous potters. You can see the whole process
- *Basketry + Woodwork*: Grain storage baskets and carved tools
- *Iron smelting*: Tools showing how Bakgatla made their own spears and hoes
- *Medicine*: Traditional herbs and healing practices

*D. MOCHUDI + MIGRATION HISTORY*
- *The Trek*: Maps of how Bakgatla moved from SA to Botswana in 1870s to escape Boers
- *Mochudi village*: Old photos of Mochudi from 1900s. It was one of the first "brick" towns in Botswana
- *School history*: Photos of the first schools and churches in Kgatleng

There’s also an *art gallery* for local Kgatleng artists.

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians, Phuthadikobo matters for 3 big reasons:

1.  *A divided nation*: The story of Kgafela II taking people to SA in 1910 is huge. Today there are 2 Bakgatla nations - one in Botswana, one in SA. The museum explains both sides.
2.  *Women’s history*: Bakgatla women are known as great potters and beer brewers. The museum has one of the best collections of traditional pottery in Botswana.
3.  *Community-run model*: This was one of the first museums in Botswana started by the tribe itself, not just government. Elders bring in artifacts and tell stories. It’s living history.

Elders say: _"Mochudi ke motse wa dikgosi"_ - "Mochudi is a village of chiefs". Phuthadikobo proves it.

*4. VISITING TODAY*
*Location*: Mochudi, next to the main kgotla. 45min from Gaborone on A1.  
*Cost*: Citizens P5, Visitors P20  
*Time needed*: 1 - 1.5 hours  
*Guides*: Bakgatla elders and youth. They’ll demonstrate pottery and explain `setapa` dance.

*Special events*: *Dikgafela Festival* in July/August. Harvest festival with dancing, beer brewing, and cultural displays. The museum is the center.  
*Gift shop*: Sells Bakgatla pottery, baskets, and beadwork.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Phuthadikobo Museum in Mochudi means 'Gather the Ashes'. It’s the museum of the Bakgatla tribe. You learn about Kgosi Kgafela who split the tribe between Botswana and South Africa in 1910. Inside you’ll see royal leopard skins, traditional pottery that Bakgatla women are famous for, and displays on initiation schools. It’s in the old kgotla cells so you feel the history. If you want to understand how one tribe lives in 2 countries, start here."

*Quick facts:*
- *Don’t miss*: The pottery gallery and the Kgafela II exhibit
- *Best time*: July for Dikgafela Festival
- *Language*: You’ll hear Sekgatla dialect - a bit different from standard Setswana
- *Nearby*: Mochudi kgotla, Oodi Weavers

*Comparison of Botswana Tribal Museums:*
- *Phuthadikobo Mochudi*: Bakgatla history, pottery, split nation
- *Khama III Serowe*: Bangwato chiefs, presidents
- *Kgosi Sechele Molepolole*: Bakwena + Livingstone
- *Nhabe Maun*: Batawana + Delta tribes
- *Supa Ngwao Francistown*: Kalanga culture

*Supa Ngwao Museum* 🇧🇼  
_Location: Francistown, North-East District_  
_“Supa Ngwao” = "Show the Culture" in Kalanga_

For people interested in culture and history, *Supa Ngwao is the home of Kalanga + Northern Botswana history*. It’s the 2nd biggest museum in Botswana after the National Museum in Gaborone.

*1. HISTORY + THE BUILDING*
*Opened*: 1986  
*Location*: Francistown CBD, on the banks of the Tati River  
*Run by*: National Museum of Botswana + Francistown City Council  
*Building*: A beautiful thatched building designed like a big Kalanga hut `ntlo`. Outside there’s a traditional village display.

*Why Francistown*: Francistown is the 2nd city and the heart of *Kalanga* land. It was also a gold mining town since 1897. So the museum covers both *people and mining history*.

*2. WHAT’S INSIDE - THE MAIN EXHIBITS*

*A. KALANGA CULTURE GALLERY*
This is the core. Kalanga are the largest non-Tswana group in Botswana.
- *Kalanga Kings*: History of the *Tjikalanga* kingdoms that came before the Ndebele and Tswana. Maps of Great Zimbabwe connections
- *Royal regalia*: Beadwork, axes, and `izibuko` royal stools
- *Initiation*: `Domba` snake dance and coming-of-age ceremonies
- *Language*: Displays on Kalanga language and proverbs. Different from Setswana

*B. BASKETRY + CRAFTS*
Francistown is famous for this.
- *Tebekane baskets*: The famous "eyelash" baskets from the Shashe and Tati area. You can see the whole process from palm leaves to finished basket
- *Pottery*: Kalanga pots for beer, water, and cooking
- *Wood carving + Beadwork*: Traditional clothes `isikhakha` and jewelry

*C. MINING + CITY HISTORY*
Francistown started as a gold rush town in 1897.
- *Gold mining*: Tools, photos, and ore from the old Monarch Mine
- *Railway history*: Francistown was the end of the railway line in 1897. Photos of the first trains
- *Old Francistown*: Photos of the city from 1900s to today

*D. OTHER NORTHERN TRIBES*
The museum also covers people of the North-East:
- *Ndebele*: Their migration into Botswana, beadwork and house painting
- *Nambya + Tswapong*: Tools, music, and stories
- *San*: Stone tools found in the area

There’s also an *art gallery* for artists from the North.

*E. OUTSIDE - LIVING VILLAGE*
- *Traditional huts*: Kalanga, Ndebele, and Tswana style huts you can walk into
- *Granaries + Kraals*: Shows how people stored food and cattle
- *Medicinal garden*: Plants used by traditional healers

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians, Supa Ngwao matters for 3 big reasons:

1.  *Kalanga identity*: For years Kalanga history was told from Tswana or Ndebele side. This museum puts Kalanga in the center. "Supa Ngwao" literally means "show us our culture".
2.  *Trade + Mining crossroads*: Francistown was where gold miners, railway workers, and traders from Zimbabwe, Zambia, and SA met. The museum shows that mix.
3.  *Basketry capital*: The "eyelash" baskets sold worldwide start here. The museum trains women and preserves the patterns.

Elders say: _"Sino sethu, si phakamise"_ - "This is ours, let us lift it up."

*4. VISITING TODAY*
*Location*: 5min walk from Francistown CBD, next to Tati River  
*Cost*: Citizens P5, Visitors P20  
*Time needed*: 2 hours  
*Guides*: Mostly Kalanga guides who explain basket patterns and history in English + Kalanga

*Special events*: *Tebekane Basket Festival* every August. Basket weavers from all over come to sell and demonstrate.  
*Gift shop*: Best place to buy authentic Kalanga baskets. Money goes to the weavers.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Supa Ngwao Museum in Francistown means 'Show the Culture'. It’s the museum of northern Botswana. The main focus is Kalanga people - their kings, language, and famous baskets with eyelash patterns. But it also tells the story of gold mining in 1897 and the railway. Outside there are real huts you can go in. If you want to understand the part of Botswana that’s NOT Tswana, you start here."

*Quick facts:*
- *Don’t miss*: The basket gallery and the living village outside
- *Best time*: August for Tebekane Basket Festival
- *Language*: You’ll hear Kalanga. It sounds like Ndebele + Shona mix
- *Nearby*: 20min from Zimbabwe border

*Comparison of Botswana Museums:*
- *National Museum Gaborone*: Whole country overview
- *Supa Ngwao Francistown*: Kalanga + mining + northern tribes
- *Phuthadikobo Mochudi*: Bakgatla
- *Khama III Serowe*: Bangwato
- *Nhabe Maun*: Batawana + Delta

*Tsodilo Hills Museum & Cultural Centre* 🇧🇼  
_Location: Tsodilo Hills, North-West District, 40km from Shorobe_  
_“Tsodilo” = "The Hills that Whisper" or "Place of Spirits" in Hambukushu_

For people interested in culture and history, *Tsodilo is the most important rock art site in Southern Africa*. UNESCO calls it *"The Louvre of the Desert"*. The museum is small, but it’s the gateway to 100,000 years of human history.

*1. HISTORY + THE PLACE*
*Designated*: UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001  
*Location*: 4 granite hills rising 400m from the flat Kalahari. About 1 hour from Maun.  
*Who lived here*: *San people* for at least *100,000 years*. Later *Bantu farmers* and *Batawana* also lived here.  
*Why it matters*: Over *4,500 rock paintings* in 400+ sites. That’s one of the highest concentrations in the world.

The Hills are sacred to San people. They believe ancestors, spirits, and the first people came from here.

*2. THE MUSEUM / CULTURAL CENTRE*
*Opened*: 2006  
*Location*: At the main gate, before you hike the hills  
*Run by*: Tsodilo Community Trust - San and Hambukushu people from nearby villages

It’s small but powerful. It’s not about glass cases. It’s about *context before you walk*.

*WHAT’S INSIDE*

*A. ROCK ART GALLERY*
- *Photos of paintings*: Close-ups of animals, people, and "shaman" figures you can’t get close to on the hill
- *Meaning*: San guides explain the 3 types of art: 
  1.  *Hunting magic* - to make animals come
    2.  *Trance art* - visions from `n/um` trance dance 
    3.  *Historical* - giraffe, elephant, and cattle as environment changed
- *Tools*: Stone tools 70,000 years old, ostrich eggshell beads, grinding stones found in the caves

*B. PEOPLE + SPIRITUALITY GALLERY*
- *San oral history*: Recordings of elders explaining creation stories. How the hills were made
- *Hambukushu + Batawana*: How later people also used the hills for rain-making ceremonies
- *Sacred sites map*: Shows "Male Hill", "Female Hill", and "Child Hill" and what each means

*C. LIVING CULTURE OUTSIDE*
- *Traditional huts*: San `ntlo` and Hambukushu houses
- *Craft shop*: Beadwork, bows, and paintings by San artists from the villages
- *Storytelling fire pit*: Where guides tell legends in the evening

*3. THE HILLS THEMSELVES - THE REAL MUSEUM*
After the museum, you hike with a *San guide*. This is required.

*4 Main Trails:*
1.  *Rhino Trail - 1 hour*: Easiest. Has the famous "Rhino painting" + Iron Age cave
2.  *Lion Trail - 3 hours*: Most paintings. "Bushman painting" site
3.  *Male Hill - 1 hour*: Highest point. Sacred site. No photos allowed on top
4.  *Female Hill - 45min*: Cave with 1700+ paintings

*What you learn on the walk:*
- How San used plants for food and medicine
- How to find water in rock cracks
- Why certain animals are painted where they are

*4. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians, Tsodilo matters for 3 big reasons:

1.  *Longest continuous human record*: 100,000 years of people living in one place. From Stone Age tools to paintings done 20 years ago.
2.  *Climate history*: Paintings show rhino and zebra when the area was wet. Then cattle when it dried. The rocks are a climate diary.
3.  *Living spirituality*: Unlike most rock art sites, San people still come here to pray and do ceremonies. It’s not dead history.

Elders say: _"Dithaba di a bua"_ - "The hills talk". If you listen, they tell you about the first people.

*5. VISITING TODAY*
*Location*: 340km from Maun. Last 40km is deep sand - 4x4 needed.  
*Cost*: Park fees P120 visitors, P40 citizens + guide P200 per group  
*Time needed*: 1 full day minimum. Overnight camping available  
*Guides*: Must be San or Hambukushu from the Trust. They are the storytellers.

*Rules*: No touching paintings, no flash photos, no climbing on art. This is a church for San people.

*Best time*: May - September. Cool and dry.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Tsodilo Hills Museum is the door to 'The Louvre of the Desert'. For 100,000 years San people painted on these rocks. There are 4,500 paintings of animals, shamans, and stories. The small museum explains what it means, then a San guide takes you to see it on the hills. It’s not just old art. The hills are still sacred today. This is where human history in Southern Africa started."

*Quick facts:*
- *Don’t miss*: The Rhino painting and the view from Male Hill
- *Age*: Paintings from 24,000 years ago to last year
- *Unique*: Only UNESCO site in Botswana managed by the local community
- *Nearby*: Stay in Shorobe or Maun

*Comparison of Botswana Heritage Sites:*
- *Tsodilo*: Rock art + San spirituality, 100,000 years
- *Supa Ngwao Francistown*: Kalanga culture + baskets
- *Phuthadikobo Mochudi*: Bakgatla kings
- *Ntsweng*: Founding of Gaborone
- *National Museum*: Overview of everything
