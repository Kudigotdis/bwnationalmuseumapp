*Ntlo & Lapa* 🇧🇼  
_The heart of a traditional Setswana home_

For people interested in culture and history, *"ntlo le lapa"* literally means *"house and yard"*. But in Botswana it means way more than that. It’s the blueprint of how Batswana lived, organized family, and showed respect for 200+ years.

*1. WHAT IS AN NTLO?*
*Ntlo* = The house / hut

*TRADITIONAL NTLO*
*Built by*: Women. Building a house was a woman’s job and a skill passed from mother to daughter.  
*Materials*: 
- *Walls*: Mud + cow dung + grass. Later bricks
- *Roof*: Grass thatch `seota` or corrugated iron today
- *Floor*: Polished cow dung `podi` - smooth and cool

*Inside layout:*
- *Seotlo*: The sacred corner where ancestors are honored. Beer `bojalwa` is poured here first
- *Diphoko*: Grinding stones for sorghum `mabele`
- *Storage*: Pots for water `mekgaba` and grain bins `dikgopana`

*Meaning*: The `ntlo` is private, female space. It’s where children are born, food is cooked, and stories are told at night.

*2. WHAT IS A LAPA?*
*Lapa* = The fenced yard / courtyard

This is the most important part. A Motswana doesn’t say "I live in a house". They say "I live in a lapa".

*TRADITIONAL LAPA LAYOUT*
A lapa is a circle of huts with a shared yard in the middle.

*Key parts:*
1.  *Lekgotla / Kgotla ya lapa*: Shaded area with tree or grass roof. Where the family meets, eats, and visitors are welcomed
2.  *Losaka*: Cattle kraal. Usually at the back. Cattle = wealth and status
3.  *Kichine*: Outdoor cooking area with 3-stone fire `dikgong tse tharo`
4.  *Ntlwana ya go tlhapa*: Bathing area
5.  *Dithako*: Granaries on stilts to keep food safe from mice

*Fence*: Made of sticks `letšwa` or thorn bushes. Keeps animals out and defines "home".

*Meaning*: The `lapa` is community space. Men sit here. Elders hold meetings here. Kids play here. It’s public + family life.

*3. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, `ntlo le lapa` shows 3 big Batswana values:

*A. BOTHO + COMMUNITY*
The circle layout means no one is in front. Everyone faces the center.  
Proverb: _"Motho ke motho ka batho"_ - "A person is a person through other people"  
The lapa forces you to live together.

*B. GENDER ROLES*
*Women*: Build and own the `ntlo`. They control the kitchen and inside.  
*Men*: Own the cattle in the `losaka` and represent the lapa outside.  
But decisions about the lapa are made together at the lekgotla.

*C. ORDER + RESPECT*
There’s a hierarchy:
`Kgosana` Chief’s lapa → Headman’s lapa → Family lapa → Individual ntlo  
Even today, when you enter a lapa you clap `go opa diatla` and greet: _"Dumela Rra/Mma"_

*4. NTLO & LAPA TODAY*
Traditional ones are still in villages. Modern ones are in cities too.

*Modern Lapa in Gaborone:*
- Brick house instead of mud hut
- But still has: *Lapa* with braai, tree, chairs. *Losaka* might be a garage
- Weekend funerals, weddings, and family meetings still happen in the lapa

*Why it survives*: Even with apartments, Batswana build a "lapa" in the backyard. Because culture lives in the yard, not just the house.

You’ll see "Lapa" in restaurant names too: _Lapa Restaurant_ = come eat outside, like family.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"When Batswana say 'ntlo le lapa', they mean 'house and yard'. The `ntlo` is the hut built by women, for private family life. The `lapa` is the fenced yard in the middle where everyone meets under a tree. Cattle kraal at the back, kitchen outside, guests greeted at the entrance. 

It’s 200 years old design. The circle means equality. The yard means community. Even today in Gaborone, people build modern houses but they still need a lapa for funerals and gatherings. To understand Batswana culture, you have to understand the lapa."

*Quick facts:*
- *Built by*: Women build ntlo. Men + women maintain lapa
- *Sacred spot*: Seotlo corner in the ntlo for ancestors
- *Greeting*: Always clap and greet before entering a lapa
- *Saying*: _"Lapa la me ke lefelo la ka la boiketlo"_ - "My lapa is my place of peace"

*See it in real life:*
- *Phuthadikobo Museum, Mochudi*: Has a model Bakgatla lapa
- *Ntsweng Heritage Site*: See stone foundations of 1800s lapa
- *Mokolodi Village Display*: Walk through a recreated lapa

*Wooden Stool* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Setulo* or *Setulo sa setso*_  
_Kalanga: *Isibuko* / *Inqola*_  
_In history: "The throne that fits in your hand"_

For people interested in culture and history, *the wooden stool in Botswana is not just for sitting*. It’s status, identity, spirituality, and law all carved into one piece of wood.

*1. WHAT IS THE TRADITIONAL STOOL?*
*Made by*: Men. Carving was a specialist skill passed from father to son.  
*Wood*: Usually *mopane*, *leadwood*, or *teak*. Hard woods that don’t break and don’t get termites.  
*Design*: 3 main types you’ll see across Botswana

*A. SETULO SA KGOSI - CHIEF'S STOOL*
- *Shape*: Low, 3 or 4 legs, wide seat. Sometimes with a backrest
- *Decoration*: Carved patterns, burned designs, and sometimes covered in *leopard skin* `nkwe`
- *Meaning*: This IS the office. A kgosi doesn’t rule without his stool. When a new chief is installed, he is "put on the stool" `go tsenngwa mo setulong`

*B. SETULO SA RRA/LAPA HEAD*
- *Shape*: Simple 3-legged stool. About knee height
- *Meaning*: Head of family. Only he sits on it in the `lapa`. Visitors get benches.  
- *Rule*: No one else sits on it without permission. It shows authority

*C. SETULO SA MOSADI - WOMAN'S STOOL*
- *Shape*: Smaller, often with a curved seat for comfort
- *Use*: For grinding `go sila`, cooking, and talking with other women
- *Meaning*: Made by her husband as a gift. Shows he respects her

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, the wooden stool shows 3 big Batswana values:

*A. AUTHORITY + LAW*
In the *kgotla*, the chief sits on his stool. Everyone else sits on the ground.  
Proverb: _"Setulo se a bua"_ - "The stool speaks". Meaning the person on the stool makes decisions.  
When cases are judged, the stool is the center. 

*B. SPIRITUALITY + ANCESTORS*
- *Ancestor stool*: A small stool is kept in the `seotlo` corner of the `ntlo`. Beer is poured on it for ancestors
- *Burial*: Important people were sometimes buried with their stool
- *Healing*: Traditional doctors `dingaka` have special stools for diagnosing

*C. IDENTITY + TRIBE*
Different groups have different styles:
- *Bangwato/Bakwena*: Simple, smooth, functional
- *Bakgatla*: More carving and patterns
- *Kalanga*: `Isibuko` stools often have 4 legs and are taller, with geometric patterns
- *San*: Low stools made from tree stumps, used in the bush

*3. HOW IT’S MADE - THE CRAFT*
1.  *Choose tree*: Must ask permission from the tree and ancestors
2.  *Carve from one log*: Traditional stools are carved from a single piece. No nails
3.  *Fire + oil*: Rubbed with fat or oil to make it dark and shiny. Takes weeks
4.  *Patterns*: Burned-in triangles, lines, and dots tell a story or clan

A good stool can last 50+ years and is passed to a son.

*4. WOODEN STOOL TODAY*
You still see them everywhere:
- *In kgotlas*: Chief still uses his stool for meetings
- *In homes*: Grandfather’s stool is in the lapa for visitors
- *Ceremonies*: Weddings, `bogwera`, funerals. Elders sit on stools
- *Art*: Modern artists in Gaborone and Mochudi now sell carved stools to tourists

*Rule you should know*: If you visit a home, don’t sit on the headman’s stool without being invited. It’s like sitting in the CEO’s chair.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"In Botswana the wooden stool `setulo` is more than furniture. For 300+ years it’s been a symbol of power. 

The chief’s stool `setulo sa kgosi` is like a crown. When he sits, the law sits.  
The family head’s stool is his authority in the `lapa`.  
Women have smaller stools for work.  
And there are ancestor stools in the house for blessings.

It’s carved from mopane by hand, no nails. Patterns tell you which tribe the carver was from. Today chiefs still use them in kgotla. If you want to understand how Batswana show respect and leadership, look at who is sitting on the stool."

*Quick facts:*
- *Material*: Mopane, Leadwood, Teak
- *Legs*: 3 legs = most common and stable on sand
- *Don’t*: Don’t step over someone’s stool. It’s disrespectful
- *Saying*: _"O dutse mo setulong sa bone"_ - "You’re sitting in their seat" = you’re taking their position

*See it in real life:*
- *Phuthadikobo Museum, Mochudi*: Has Bakgatla chief stools with beadwork
- *Khama III Museum, Serowe*: Bangwato royal stools
- *Supa Ngwao, Francistown*: Kalanga `isibuko` stools
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: Collection from all tribes

*Koloi ya Tonki* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: "Donkey Cart"_  
_In history: "The Land Rover of the village"_

For people interested in culture and history, *koloi ya tonki was the engine of rural Botswana for 100+ years*. Before tar roads and bakkies, if something needed to move — water, wood, people, crops — the donkey cart did it.

*1. WHAT IS A KOLOI YA TONKI?*
*Koloi* = vehicle/cart  
*Tonki* = donkey

*THE CART*
*Built by*: Local carpenters, often in the village  
*Made of*: 
- *Wheels*: Wooden spokes with rubber from old car tyres nailed on
- *Body*: Wooden planks, sometimes with metal sheet
- *Axle*: Car axle from a scrapped vehicle  
- *Color*: Usually painted blue, green, or left natural. Each family had their color

*Pulled by*: 2 or 4 donkeys. Donkeys `ditonki` are loved because they don’t get sick easily, eat anything, and can go without water.

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, koloi ya tonki shows how Batswana survived and moved for generations:

*A. TRANSPORT + ECONOMY 1900s - 1980s*
This was the main transport in villages.
- *Water*: 200L drums to and from the borehole
- *Firewood*: Women cut wood, load the cart, sell in town
- *Crops*: Take sorghum `mabele` and maize to the mill
- *Building*: Bricks, sand, and poles for building `ntlo`
- *People*: Took kids to school, elders to kgotla, and family to funerals

Proverb: _"Fa koloi e sa tsamaye, lapa le a swela"_ - "If the cart doesn’t move, the home goes hungry"

*B. STATUS + IDENTITY*
- *Wealth*: Having donkeys + a cart meant you were a hard worker. "O na le ditonki" = "You have donkeys" = you’re okay
- *Work*: Young boys learned responsibility by herding donkeys and driving the cart
- *Community*: Neighbors would borrow your cart. You’d help each other during ploughing season

*C. SOUND OF THE VILLAGE*
Every village had a sound: _creak-creak_ of wooden wheels on sand at 5am. You knew it was time to fetch water.

*3. HOW IT WORKED - DAILY LIFE*
*Morning 4am*: Boy wakes up, catches donkeys, yokes them  
*Morning*: 2-3 trips to borehole for water. 20L tins `dinkgo`  
*Midday*: Go to lands `masimo` to bring back crops  
*Afternoon*: Take firewood to sell in town  
*Evening*: Unyoke, feed donkeys, park cart under a tree

The driver sits on the front with a stick `thobane` and shouts _"Hiya! Hiya!"_ to the donkeys.

*4. KOLOI YA TONKI TODAY*
Cars and bakkies replaced most carts, but they’re still everywhere in villages.

*Why they’re still used:*
1.  *Cheap*: No petrol. Donkeys eat grass
2.  *Sand*: Works better than cars on deep village sand
3.  *Jobs*: Many women still run "cart businesses" - delivering water and wood

*New uses:*
- *Tourism*: Safari lodges in Maun and Kasane use decorated carts for cultural tours
- *Art*: Painted carts are now in museums and cultural festivals
- *Waste collection*: Some villages use carts for recycling

*Rule*: On the road, donkey carts have right of way in rural areas. Beep and wait.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Koloi ya tonki is the donkey cart. For most of the 1900s this was how Batswana moved things. 

Two wooden wheels, pulled by 2 donkeys. Women used it for water and firewood. Men used it for crops and building. Boys grew up learning to drive it.

It’s not just transport. It meant you could survive. No petrol, no mechanic needed. Just grass and water for the donkeys. 

Today you’ll still see them in every village in Botswana. And when you hear _creak-creak_ on a dirt road, that’s history still working."

*Quick facts:*
- *Speed*: Walks about 5km per hour
- *Load*: Can carry 500kg - 1 ton
- *Life*: A good cart lasts 20 years. Donkeys live 25-30 years
- *Saying*: _"Tanki e ja moweng"_ - "The donkey eats the wind" = it’s cheap to run

*See it in real life:*
- *Supa Ngwao Museum, Francistown*: Has an old cart in the living village
- *Phuthadikobo Museum, Mochudi*: Photos of 1950s carts
- *Any village*: Go to a borehole at 6am and you’ll see 10 carts in line
- *Maun Cultural Festival*: Decorated carts in parades

*Comparison:*
- *Bakkies*: Fast, but need petrol
- *Koloi ya tonki*: Slow, but never breaks down and feeds your family

*The Daimler Presidential Car* 🇧🇼  
_Botswana's "Rolls-Royce of the Presidency"_  

For people interested in culture and history, *Botswana’s Daimler is not just a car. It’s a symbol of independence, dignity, and the "modest luxury" that defined the first presidents.*

*1. WHICH DAIMLER? THE STORY*
Botswana has used *Daimler limousines* as the official Presidential State Car since *1966*.

*The most famous one:*
*Daimler DS420 Limousine*  
*Year*: 1969 - 1970s model  
*Color*: Black, with Botswana flag and Presidential Standard on the fenders  
*Given by*: The British Government as an Independence gift in 1966

*Why Daimler*: In the 1960s, Daimler was the official car of the British Royal Family. Queen Elizabeth II used Daimlers. So when Britain gave Botswana independence, a Daimler was the gift that said: _"You are now a sovereign state."_

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, the Daimler shows 3 big ideas about early Botswana:

*A. INDEPENDENCE + DIGNITY*
*30 September 1966*: Sir Seretse Khama drove through Gaborone in the Daimler during Independence Day.  
People lined the streets. It was the first time Batswana saw "their" president in "their" official car.  
Message: We are poor, but we are a nation with respect.

*B. "MODEST LUXURY" - BOTSWANA STYLE*
Sir Seretse and Sir Quett Masire were famous for being simple. They didn’t have palaces.  
But the Daimler was different. It was only used for:  
1.  *State functions*: Opening Parliament, Independence Day
2.  *Receiving Heads of State*: When other presidents visited
3.  *Funerals*: State funerals

Rule: The president would use a normal Toyota or Land Cruiser for daily work. The Daimler only came out for "the nation". That showed _botho_ - don’t show off.

*C. CONTINUITY*
Every president since 1966 has used a Daimler for state events:
- *Sir Seretse Khama* 1966-1980
- *Sir Quett Masire* 1980-1998  
- *President Mogae* 1998-2008
- *President Khama* 2008-2018
- *President Masisi* 2018-2024
- *President Boko* 2024-now

The current one is a newer *Daimler Limousine*, still black, still maintained by the State.

*3. WHAT’S SPECIAL ABOUT THE CAR*
*Made by*: Daimler, UK. Built on a Jaguar base but with Daimler grille and "fluted" rear.  
*Features for 1960s*:
- Partition between driver and president
- Flags: Botswana flag + Presidential Standard 
- Velvet seats
- Flag poles built into the fenders
- Used for open-top parades with the roof down

*Number plate*: Usually *B1* or *P1* - "President 1"

*4. WHERE YOU CAN SEE IT TODAY*
The original 1969 Daimler is not used daily anymore. It’s preserved.

*Places:*
1.  *Botswana National Museum, Gaborone*: Sometimes displayed during Independence exhibitions. Ask for the "State Car" section
2.  *Independence Day - 30 Sept*: The current Daimler is used in the parade at National Stadium
3.  *State House, Gaborone*: Garaged there. You can see it during Open Day events

It’s maintained by the *Presidential Motor Pool*. Mechanics are trained to keep the old engine running.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Botswana’s Daimler Presidential Car is the black limousine given by Britain in 1966 when we got independence. 

Sir Seretse Khama rode in it on Independence Day. It was made by Daimler - the same brand the Queen used. 

But here’s the Botswana part: Our presidents only used it for national events. For daily work they drove normal cars. That showed we value dignity, not luxury. 

Today it’s still used for State Opening of Parliament and to welcome other presidents. The car connects every president from Seretse Khama to today. It’s our moving piece of independence history."

*Quick facts:*
- *Gift*: 1966 Independence gift from UK
- *Model*: Daimler DS420 Limousine
- *Color*: Always black
- *Use*: State functions only
- *Meaning*: Sovereignty + continuity + modesty

*See it in real life:*
- *30 September*: Independence Day parade, Gaborone National Stadium
- *State Opening of Parliament*: February each year
- *National Museum*: Call ahead to ask if the 1969 Daimler is on display

*Comparison:*
- *South Africa*: Uses BMW 7-Series 
- *Zimbabwe*: Uses Mercedes S-Class
- *Botswana*: Uses Daimler - older, British, about tradition

*Mokoro* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Mokoro* - plural *mekoro*_  
_English: Dugout canoe_  
_In history: "The legs of the Delta"_

For people interested in culture and history, *the mokoro is the soul of the Okavango Delta*. For the *Bayei people* and others in Ngamiland, it’s not just a boat. It’s transport, livelihood, home, and identity carved from one tree.

*1. WHAT IS A MOKORO?*
*Made from*: One single *Sausage Tree* `mokolwane` or *Ebony* `motsokwane` tree trunk  
*How*: A tree is felled, hollowed out with an axe `selepe` and fire, then shaped  
*Size*: 4m - 6m long. Fits 2 passengers + 1 poler  
*Color*: Natural wood, darkened by water and oil

*How it moves*: Not paddled. It’s *poled*. The poler stands at the back with a long pole `motswi` and pushes against the riverbed.

*Rule*: The mokoro glides silently. That’s why it’s perfect for the Delta.

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, the mokoro shows 3 big things about Okavango life:

*A. BAYEI PEOPLE - "PEOPLE OF THE RIVER"*
For 1000+ years the *Bayei* have lived in the Delta. They don’t walk much. They pole.  
Proverb: _"Fa o sa tsebe go tsamaisa mokoro, ga o itse go phela"_ - "If you don’t know how to pole a mokoro, you don’t know how to live"

*Daily life on mokoro:*
- *Fishing*: Cast nets and fish traps `matlaba`
- *Water*: Fetch water from clean channels
- *School*: Kids pole to school on islands
- *Funerals + Weddings*: Mekoro form a procession on the water
- *Hunting*: Historically for hippo and croc. Now only for photos

*B. SURVIVAL + SMART DESIGN*
Why a dugout and not a flat boat?
1.  *Shallow water*: Okavango is only knee to waist deep. The mokoro floats in 15cm of water
2.  *Reeds + papyrus*: Pointed ends cut through vegetation silently
3.  *One tree*: No nails, no glue. 100% local materials
4.  *Stable*: Wide bottom so it doesn’t tip with tourists and luggage

*C. SPIRITUALITY*
The tree gives its life so people can live. Before cutting, Bayei elders ask permission from the tree and ancestors.  
After a mokoro cracks and sinks, parts of it are used for firewood or left to become part of the Delta again. Nothing wasted.

*3. HOW IT’S MADE - THE CRAFT*
This is men’s work, passed father to son.

1.  *Choose tree*: Must be straight, no branches. Takes 2 days to fell
2.  *Burn + Axe*: Fire is used to soften wood, then chopped out. Takes 1 week
3.  *Shape*: Narrow front, flat bottom, slightly raised ends
4.  *Oil*: Rubbed with animal fat to make it waterproof

A good mokoro lasts 10-15 years if looked after.

*4. MOKORO TODAY*
There are 2 types now:

*A. TRADITIONAL WOODEN MOKORO*
Still used in villages like Seronga, Beetsha, and Shakawe.  
For real life: water, fishing, going to the lands `masimo`

*B. FIBERGLASS MOKORO*
Since the 1990s, for tourism.  
*Why*: Doesn’t need a tree to be cut. Lighter, doesn’t crack.  
*Same shape*: So it still poles the same and doesn’t damage the Delta.  
This is what you ride on safari in Maun.

*The poler `mokoro driver`*: Still the most important job. They read water, know every channel name, and tell stories. Many are now guides earning a living from tourism.

*Rule for tourists*: Sit still, don’t lean, and listen. The silence is the point.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"The mokoro is the dugout canoe of Botswana. For the Bayei people of the Okavango Delta, it’s been the only way to move for 1000 years.

It’s carved from one tree. You don’t paddle it, you pole it while standing. It’s so light it floats in 15cm of water and so quiet you can glide past hippos.

It carried people to school, to the fields, to fetch water. Today wooden ones are still used in villages, and fiberglass ones are used for safaris so we don’t cut more trees.

To understand the Delta, you have to understand the mokoro. Because without it, there is no Okavango."

*Quick facts:*
- *Material*: Mokolwane tree, now also fiberglass
- *Speed*: Walks pace - about 4km per hour
- *Capacity*: 2 people + guide + bags
- *Sound*: Silence. Just water dripping from the pole
- *Saying*: _"Metsi a tsamaisa mokoro"_ - "Water moves the mokoro" = Go with life

*Experience it:*
- *Maun*: Day trips and overnight mokoro safaris. Best in June - August during flood
- *Nhabe Museum, Maun*: Has a real wooden mokoro on display + Bayei fishing tools
- *Delta villages*: Seronga, Gumare. See villagers using them daily

*Comparison:*
- *South Africa*: Flat-bottom boats with motors
- *Botswana*: Mokoro. Silent, human-powered, no fuel

*Lekgetlo* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Lekgetlo* - plural *dikgetlo*_  
_English: Fish scoop / Hand net_  
_In history: "The kitchen basket of the river"_

For people interested in culture and history, *lekgetlo is the oldest fishing tool in Botswana*. Before rods and nets, women and children in the Okavango, Chobe, and Limpopo used the lekgetlo to feed the family. It’s simple, but it’s genius.

*1. WHAT IS A LEKGETLO?*
*Made by*: Women. Weaving was a woman’s skill passed from mother to daughter  
*Materials*:
- *Frame*: Flexible sticks from *moretlwa* or *mokoba* tree. Bent into a circle or oval
- *Net*: Palm leaf strips `lehlaka` or reed `lehlaka la noka`. Woven tight
- *Handle*: 1m - 2m long wooden pole tied to the frame

*Shape*: Like a big shallow basket on a stick. Some are round, some are D-shaped.

*How it works*: You push it through shallow water, grass, and reeds to scoop fish, tadpoles, and water insects.

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, lekgetlo shows 3 big things about life by the water:

*A. WOMEN'S WORK + FOOD SECURITY*
Fishing with lekgetlo was *women and children’s work*.  
*When*: Early morning and late afternoon when water is calm  
*Where*: Shallow floodplains, edges of pans, and river banks  
*Who ate it*: Small fish `dihlapi tse dinnye` were dried `go ebediwa` and stored. Big protein source during dry season

Proverb: _"Mosadi yo o nang le lekgetlo ga a lale a tshwerwe ke tlala"_ - "A woman with a lekgetlo does not sleep hungry"

*B. KNOWLEDGE OF WATER*
To use lekgetlo well you must know:
1.  *Where fish hide*: In grass, under lily pads, in mud
2.  *Seasons*: After floods when fish are in shallow pools
3.  *Tides of the Delta*: When water rises and falls

Elders could "read" the water by the ripples. The lekgetlo was an extension of their hand.

*C. COMMUNITY + TEACHING*
- *Groups*: Women went in groups of 5-10. Singing while scooping
- *Sharing*: Catch was shared in the `lapa`. Nothing wasted
- *Teaching*: Girls got their first small lekgetlo at age 6. Learn patience and skill

*3. HOW IT’S MADE - THE CRAFT*
Takes 1-2 days. 

1.  *Cut sticks*: Soak them so they bend without breaking
2.  *Weave*: Over-under pattern with palm strips. Holes small enough to trap fish
3.  *Tie handle*: With bark rope `mogoga`
4.  *Harden*: Smoke it over fire so it lasts in water

A good lekgetlo lasts 1-2 seasons. When it breaks, materials go back to the earth.

*4. LEKGETLO TODAY*
*Still used*: In Okavango villages like Seronga, Shakawe, and along the Chobe.  
*Why*: Free, no license needed, and targets small fish without harming big fish.

*2 uses now:*
1.  *Subsistence*: Families still use it for food
2.  *Culture + Tourism*: Demonstrated at Nhabe Museum and cultural villages for tourists

*Rule*: In the Delta, you can only use traditional tools like lekgetlo in community areas. Motors and big nets are for commercial fishing.

*Modern version*: Some use mosquito netting instead of reeds. Same shape.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Lekgetlo is the traditional fish scoop of Botswana. It’s a basket on a stick, woven by women from reeds and tree branches.

For hundreds of years, women in the Okavango and Chobe used it. They wade in shallow water and scoop. It catches small fish that you dry for the dry season.

It’s not about sport. It’s about survival and knowledge. To use it you must know where fish hide, how the river moves, and when to go.

Today women still make them the same way. In museums you’ll see them next to fish traps and drying racks. The lekgetlo shows how Batswana lived with the river, not against it."

*Quick facts:*
- *Used by*: Women and children
- *Target*: Small fish, frogs, water bugs for bait
- *Best time*: After floods, May - August
- *Regions*: Okavango, Chobe, Limpopo, Boteti
- *Saying*: _"Go kgela ka lekgetlo"_ - "To scoop with lekgetlo" = to do something carefully and patiently

*See it in real life:*
- *Nhabe Museum, Maun*: Full display of Bayei fishing tools including lekgetlo
- *Delta villages*: Watch women use them at the river edge early morning
- *Phuthadikobo Museum, Mochudi*: Has examples from Kgatleng rivers

*Comparison of Traditional Fishing Tools:*
- *Lekgetlo*: Scoop net for shallow water. Women’s tool
- *Mothopo*: Fish trap made of reeds
- *Letholo*: Spear for big fish. Men’s tool
- *Mosako*: Fishing line

*Fishing Net* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Letlowa la go tshwara dihlapi* or just *Letlowa* - plural *matlowa*_  
_In history: "The net that feeds the village"_

For people interested in culture and history, *the fishing net in Botswana is about community, seasons, and knowing the river*. Unlike rods for sport, nets were for feeding families. Different groups had different nets for different waters.

### *1. TYPES OF TRADITIONAL FISHING NETS*
Botswana has 2 main water systems, so 2 main net styles developed.

#### *A. LETLOWA LA GO GOGA - DRAG NET / SEINE NET*
*Who used it*: Men in groups, along the *Chobe, Limpopo, and Shashe rivers*  
*Made of*: Cotton twine or bark rope `mogoga`. Floats `diphaphathi` from reeds, weights `maswika` from stones  
*How*: 2 men hold each end and drag it through shallow water. 10-20 people sometimes pull together  
*Catches*: Big fish like bream `matswi`, catfish `mokolobetwe`, tiger fish

*Cultural rule*: This was communal work. After the catch, fish was divided. _“Letlowa la batho ba le mmogo”_ - "A net of people together"

#### *B. LETLOWA LA GO EMISA - GILL NET*
*Who used it*: Men, left overnight in the *Okavango Delta and Lake Ngami*  
*Made of*: Finer twine. Set between 2 poles and fish get caught by their gills  
*How*: Set in channels in the evening, checked in the morning  
*Catches*: Medium fish

#### *C. MOSAKO - HAND LINE / FISHING LINE*
Not a net, but important. One hook, one line. Used by boys and for sport.

#### *D. RELATED: LEKGETLO - FISH SCOOP*
*Who used it*: Women and children in shallow water. See previous message.  
A net on a stick. For small fish `dihlapi tse dinnye`

### *2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, `letlowa` shows 3 big Batswana values:

#### *A. SEASONS + LAW*
Batswana never fished year-round. There were rules:
- *Go thibelwa*: Fishing banned during breeding season, usually Dec - Feb when rivers flood
- *Sacred pools*: Some pools `madiba` were for ancestors only. No nets allowed
- *Chief’s permission*: In the past you needed permission from the `kgosi` to set big nets

Proverb: _"Fa o fetsa ditlapi tsotlhe, bana ba gago ba tla ja eng?"_ - "If you finish all the fish, what will your children eat?"

#### *B. COMMUNITY + SHARING*
Drag net fishing needed 10+ people. 
- *Young men*: Pulled the net
- *Elders*: Stood and directed where to go
- *Women*: Waited on the bank to clean and smoke the fish `go ebedisa dihlapi`

The catch was shared in the `lapa`. Big fish to elders first.

#### *C. KNOWLEDGE*
To use a net you had to know:
1.  *Dihlapi*: 30+ names for fish in Setswana. Where each lives
2.  *Metsi*: How the river moves, where deep holes are
3.  *Diti*: Making knots that don’t slip in water

### *3. HOW NETS WERE MADE*
*Before 1900s*: 100% local
- *Rope*: Bark of `moretlwa` tree, twisted
- *Floats*: Bundles of reeds
- *Weights*: Stones tied with grass

*After 1900s*: Cotton thread and nylon from traders. Stronger, but same knots.

A big drag net took 3 months to make. It was a family treasure passed down.

### *4. FISHING NETS TODAY*
*Law now*: Department of Fisheries regulates nets. 
- *Mesh size*: Must be big so small fish escape
- *License*: Needed for gill nets
- *Banned*: Monofilament nets in some areas

*2 uses now:*
1.  *Subsistence*: Villages along Chobe, Zambezi, and Okavango still use traditional nets with permits
2.  *Commercial*: Licensed fishermen supply markets in Maun, Kasane, Gaborone

*Tourism*: You can watch net fishing demonstrations at *Nhabe Museum, Maun* and in Delta villages.

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"In Setswana we call a fishing net *letlowa*. 

There were 2 main types. The *drag net* `letlowa la go goga` pulled by many men in the river. And the *gill net* `letlowa la go emisa` set overnight.

Nets were not just for food. They were about law. Chiefs banned fishing in breeding season. The catch was shared by the whole village. 

Women had a smaller version called *lekgetlo* - a scoop net on a stick.

Today Botswana still uses nets, but with rules to protect fish. If you go to Maun or Kasane you’ll see fishermen mending nets in the morning, same as 100 years ago."

#### *Quick facts:*
- *Setswana words*: 
 - *Letlowa* = net
 - *Dihlapi* = fish
 - *Go tshwara dihlapi* = to catch fish
 - *Go ebedisa* = to smoke/dry fish
- *Best season*: May - September, when water is low and fish gather
- *Regions*: Okavango, Chobe, Zambezi, Limpopo, Boteti
- *Don’t*: Don’t fish during spawning. It’s disrespect to the river

#### *See it in real life:*
- *Nhabe Museum, Maun*: Has old cotton drag nets + Bayei fishing tools
- *Chobe River, Kasane*: Early morning you’ll see fishermen setting nets
- *Okavango villages*: Seronga, Beetsha. Ask for a demonstration

#### *Comparison:*
- *Lekgetlo*: Small scoop net. Women, shallow water
- *Letlowa*: Big net. Men, deep water, community
- *Rod*: New, for sport

*Seretlho* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Seretlho* - plural *diseretlho*_  
_English: Traditional snare / trap_  
_In history: "The patient hunter's tool"_

For people interested in culture and history, *seretlho was the main hunting tool for Batswana for centuries*. Before guns, before dogs, men used knowledge, rope, and patience. The seretlho fed families and taught boys discipline.

### *1. WHAT IS A SERETLHO?*
*Seretlho* = A snare or trap made to catch animals alive by the leg, neck, or body.

*Made by*: Men and boys. Snare-making was taught from father to son from age 10.  
*Materials - 100% bush*:
- *Rope*: Twisted bark from `moretlwa` or `mokoba` tree. Called `mogoga`
- *Pegs*: Hard wood `mosetlha` to anchor it
- *Trigger*: Thin sticks and grass. No metal

*Types:*
1.  *Seretlho sa molala* - Neck snare: Set on animal paths. Tightens when animal walks through
2.  *Seretlho sa leoto* - Leg snare: For small animals like hare `mmutla` and duiker `phuti`
3.  *Seretlho sa noga* - Bird snare: Using sticky gum on branches

### *2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, seretlho shows 3 big Batswana values:

#### *A. KNOWLEDGE + PATIENCE*
You couldn’t just set a seretlho anywhere. You had to know:
1.  *Ditsela tsa diphoofolo* - Animal paths. Where duiker, steenbok `thutswa` walk every day
2.  *Moya* - Wind direction. Animals smell you
3.  *Dinako* - Time. Check snares at dawn, before sun spoils the meat

Proverb: _"Yo o potlakileng o ja seretlho se se se nang le sepe"_ - "The one who rushes eats from an empty snare"  
Meaning: Patience brings food.

#### *B. LAW + RESPECT*
There were strict rules from the `kgosi`:
- *No seretlho in breeding season*: Let animals multiply
- *No seretlho near waterholes in drought*: Animals must drink
- *Check daily*: If you left an animal to suffer, you were fined
- *Share*: Meat was shared in the `lapa`. Head to the elder

This was conservation 200 years before the word existed.

#### *C. BOYHOOD + SKILLS*
Setting seretlho was how boys became men.
- *First snare*: At age 10-12, for birds
- *First catch*: You had to give it to your mother. She would cook and praise you
- *Tracking*: You learned to read footprints `dinao` and droppings `makgapetsana`

### *3. HOW IT’S SET - THE CRAFT*
1.  *Find path*: Look for fresh dung and tracks
2.  *Clear*: Make a small tunnel with branches so animal must go through the loop
3.  *Set loop*: Loop size matters. Too big = animal escapes. Too small = doesn’t work
4.  *Camouflage*: Cover with leaves and dust. No human smell
5.  *Wait*: Check next morning

A good hunter had 20-30 seretlho set at once.

### *4. SERETLHO TODAY*
*Law*: Since the 1960s, most snaring is *illegal* in Botswana. Because of poaching and cruelty.  
*Why banned*: Guns and commercial hunting replaced it. Also to protect endangered animals.

*But it’s still part of culture:*
1.  *Museums*: *Nhabe Museum Maun*, *National Museum Gaborone* have displays of traditional seretlho
2.  *Cultural villages*: Demonstrations for tourists showing how it was made - without catching animals
3.  *Metaphor*: People still say _"O wetse mo seretlhong"_ - "You’ve fallen into a snare" = you’re in trouble

*Legal use*: Only very small mouse/rat traps called seretlho are still used around homes.

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Seretlho is the traditional snare of Botswana. It’s a rope trap made from tree bark.

For 300 years, men and boys set seretlho to catch duiker, hare, and birds. But it wasn’t random. You had to know animal paths, the wind, and the seasons. 

The kgosi made laws: no snares in breeding season, check them daily, share the meat. 

It taught boys patience. _‘Yo o potlakileng o ja seretlho se se se nang le sepe’_ - ‘The impatient one eats from an empty snare.’

Today snaring is illegal to protect wildlife. But in museums you can still see how clever the design was. The seretlho shows how Batswana lived with the bush, using skill instead of force."

#### *Quick facts:*
- *Material*: `Mogoga` bark rope, wood pegs
- *Target*: Small antelope, hare, guinea fowl `kgaka`
- *Best time*: Winter `mariha`, May - August when grass is low
- *Skill*: Tracking, knot tying, patience
- *Saying*: _"Seretlho ga se tshware phofu e e sa feteng"_ - "A snare doesn’t catch an animal that doesn’t pass" = opportunity matters

#### *See it in real life:*
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: Hunting and gathering section
- *Nhabe Museum, Maun*: Bayei and San hunting tools
- *Mokolodi Nature Reserve*: Cultural tour shows how seretlho was set

#### *Comparison:*
- *Seretlho*: Snare. Passive, patient, for meat
- *Lerumo*: Spear. Active hunting. Men’s weapon
- *Mosako*: Fishing line

*Lerumo la Kuba* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Lerumo la Kuba*_  
_English: "The spear of the hump" / Short throwing spear_  
_In history: "The warrior's last word"_

For people interested in culture and history, *lerumo la kuba is the classic Batswana spear*. It’s not the long cattle-herding stick. This one was for war, hunting, and showing status. "Kuba" means the hump or raised part — referring to the thick, heavy blade.

### *1. WHAT IS LERUMO LA KUBA?*
*Lerumo* = Spear  
*Kuba* = Hump/raised blade

#### *THE SPEAR*
*Made by*: Men, specifically blacksmiths `badiri ba tshipi`. Smithing was a sacred skill.  
*Parts:*
1.  *Kuba / Lehare* - Blade: Wide, heavy iron blade with a "hump" in the middle. 20-30cm long. Forged from iron
2.  *Motshamo* - Socket: Where blade fits into the shaft
3.  *Mosamo* - Shaft: Hardwood from `motsentsetla` or `mokoba`. 1.2m - 1.5m long
4.  *Kgwele* - Butt: Sometimes had an iron spike for balance or for stabbing

*Total length*: About arm length. Shorter than European spears. Made for close combat.

*2 types:*
- *La go latlha* - Throwing spear: Lighter, balanced to throw
- *La go hlaba* - Stabbing spear: Heavier kuba, used in hand-to-hand

### *2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, lerumo la kuba shows 3 big things about old Botswana:

#### *A. WAR + DEFENSE 1700s - 1880s*
This was before guns. Every `regiment` `mephato` of young men had lerumo.  
*How it was used*:
1.  *Throw first*: Soften the enemy from 10m away
2.  *Close in*: Then fight with the same spear in hand
3.  *Formation*: Warriors fought in a circle `seboka`, protecting each other

*Famous use*: Wars between Bangwato, Bakwena, and Ndebele. Also defense against raiders.

Proverb: _"Lerumo ga le bue, le a tlhaba"_ - "A spear doesn’t talk, it stabs" = Actions matter

#### *B. HUNTING + PROVISION*
For big game: *kudu `phofu`*, *eland `tholo`*, *buffalo `nare`*  
The heavy kuba blade could break bone. Hunters threw it, then tracked the animal.  
A man who could make his own lerumo and bring meat was respected.

#### *C. STATUS + RITUAL*
- *Initiation `bogwera`*: Boys were given their first lerumo when they became men
- *Chief's guard*: Royal guards carried decorated lerumo with beadwork on the shaft
- *Kgotla*: Elders would lean on their lerumo while judging cases. It showed authority
- *Burial*: A warrior was sometimes buried with his lerumo

### *3. HOW IT’S MADE - THE CRAFT*
This was men’s sacred work. 

1.  *Mining*: Dig iron ore `leswika la tshipi`
2.  *Smelting*: 3-day fire in a clay furnace `seago`. Women sang to keep the fire alive
3.  *Forging*: Hammer the hot iron into the wide "kuba" shape. The hump gave it weight
4.  *Hardening*: Quench in water. Some smiths had secret mixtures
5.  *Shaft*: Carve hardwood, fit the blade, bind with wet animal sinew that shrinks tight

A good smith `mmegi` was as important as a doctor.

### *4. LERUMO LA KUBA TODAY*
*Guns replaced it* in the late 1800s. But it’s still alive in culture:

1.  *Ceremony*: At `bogwera`, `bojale`, and weddings, men carry lerumo as tradition
2.  *Kgotla*: Headmen still have a ceremonial lerumo standing by their chair
3.  *Dance*: Traditional `setapa` and `borankana` dancers carry lerumo
4.  *Museums*: *National Museum Gaborone*, *Khama III Museum Serowe*, *Phuthadikobo Mochudi* all have collections

*Rule*: Never touch another man’s lerumo without permission. It’s like touching his name.

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Lerumo la kuba is the traditional Batswana spear. 'Kuba' means the hump - that wide, heavy iron blade in the middle.

It was about 1.3m long. Warriors used it 2 ways: throw it, then fight with it. Before guns, this was how Batswana defended villages and hunted big animals.

But it was more than a weapon. A man got his first lerumo at initiation. Chiefs used it in kgotla. Blacksmiths who made them were respected like doctors.

Today we don’t fight with it. But you’ll still see lerumo at cultural dances, in museums, and standing next to a chief. It means: 'I am a Motswana man.'"

#### *Quick facts:*
- *Material*: Iron blade, hardwood shaft, animal sinew
- *Length*: 1.2m - 1.5m
- *Made by*: Blacksmiths `badiri ba tshipi`
- *Use*: War, hunting, ceremony, status
- *Saying*: _"O tshwere lerumo ka fa tlase"_ - "You’re holding the spear low" = you’re ready for trouble

#### *See it in real life:*
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: Has Ndebele and Tswana war spears
- *Khama III Museum, Serowe*: Bangwato royal spears
- *Phuthadikobo Museum, Mochudi*: Bakgatla blacksmith display
- *Cultural festivals*: Maitisong, Dithubaruba. Watch setapa dancers

#### *Comparison:*
- *Lerumo la kuba*: Short, heavy, for war and hunting. Batswana
- *Iklwa*: Zulu stabbing spear. Longer
- *Segai*: San throwing spear. Lighter, for small game
- *Molamu*: Herding stick. Not a weapon

*Moropa o Mogolo* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Moropa o mogolo* - plural *dimeropa tse dikgolo*_  
_English: "The Big Drum"_  
_In history: "The voice of the nation"_

For people interested in culture and history, *moropa o mogolo is not just a drum. It’s the microphone, newspaper, and church bell of old Botswana combined into one.* When it sounded, everyone knew something important was happening.

### *1. WHAT IS MOROPA O MOGOLO?*
*Moropa* = Drum  
*O mogolo* = The big one

#### *THE DRUM*
*Made by*: Men. Carving the body. Women decorated it.  
*Materials*:
- *Body*: Hollowed trunk of *mokolwane* sausage tree or *motsentsetla*. 1m - 1.5m tall
- *Skin*: Cow hide `letlalo la kgomo`. Stretched and tied with rope `mogoga`
- *Sticks*: 2 heavy wooden beaters `dithupa tsa go opa`

*Sound*: Deep, low _dum-dum_ that travels 5km across the flats. You feel it in your chest before you hear it.

*2 main types:*
1.  *Moropa wa kgotla* - Chief’s drum: Kept at the kgosi’s lapa
2.  *Moropa wa molelo* - War/festival drum: Bigger, louder

### *2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, moropa o mogolo was the communication system for 300+ years:

#### *A. NEWS + MEETINGS*
Before phones, the drum called people.  
*Different beats meant different things:*
- *Dum... Dum... Dum*: Slow = "Kgotla meeting now. Come."
- *Dum-dum-dum-dum*: Fast = "Danger! Enemy coming!"
- *Dum dum - pause - dum dum*: "Funeral. Gather."

Proverb: _"Moropa o bua le batho botlhe"_ - "The drum speaks to all people"

#### *B. CEREMONY + SPIRITUALITY*
- *Bogwera & Bojale*: Initiation. Drums played for days and nights
- *Pula ceremony*: Rain-making. Chief beats the drum to call ancestors
- *Weddings & Funerals*: Different rhythms for joy and mourning
- *Independence*: 30 Sept 1966, moropa o mogolo was beaten all night

#### *C. WAR + UNITY*
When Bangwato, Bakwena, or Bangwaketse went to war, the big drum led the `mephato` regiments.  
It set the marching pace. It gave courage.  
_“Fa moropa o opa, pelo e a nonofa”_ - "When the drum beats, the heart becomes brave"

### *3. WHO BEATS IT - THE CRAFT*
*Opa moropa* = Drum beater. This was an important job.

*Rules:*
- *Only certain men*: Chosen by the chief. Had to be strong and know the codes
- *Training*: Boys learned from age 15. Different rhythms for different messages
- *Sacred*: You didn’t beat it for fun. It was disrespectful
- *Women*: Didn’t beat it, but they sang and danced to it

A good beater could "talk" on the drum. People in the next village would know: "Chief is calling" or "There’s a wedding".

### *4. MOROPA O MOGOLO TODAY*
You still see and hear it everywhere:

1.  *Kgotla meetings*: Village kgotla still starts with 3 drum beats
2.  *Independence Day*: 30 September parade. Biggest drums lead
3.  *Cultural festivals*: Maitisong, Dithubaruba. Dance groups use it
4.  *Tourism*: Cultural villages in Maun and Gaborone demonstrate it
5.  *Church*: Some churches use smaller versions

*Modern version*: Some are now made with fiberglass so they don’t crack. Same sound.

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Moropa o mogolo is the big drum of Botswana. 

It’s carved from one tree with cow skin on top. About as tall as a man.

For 300 years this was how Batswana sent messages. 3 slow beats = meeting at kgotla. Fast beats = danger. 

It was also for ceremonies. Initiations, rain-making, funerals, independence. When the drum beat, the whole village stopped and listened.

Today we have phones, but we still beat the drum first. Because the drum doesn’t just give information. It gives feeling. It says 'this is important. We are together.'"

#### *Quick facts:*
- *Height*: 1m - 1.5m tall
- *Material*: Mokolwane wood, cow hide, mogoga rope
- *Played by*: Chosen men, 2 beaters
- *Sound*: Can be heard 3-5km away
- *Saying*: _"O re o utlwile moropa?"_ - "Did you hear the drum?" = Did you get the message?

#### *Hear + See it:*
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: Has 1800s chief’s drums
- *Phuthadikobo Museum, Mochudi*: Bakgatla war drums
- *30 September*: Independence Day, National Stadium. Don’t miss it
- *Cultural villages*: Mokolodi, Manyana. They let you try beating it

#### *Comparison:*
- *Moropa o mogolo*: Big, deep, for nation. Chief’s drum
- *Moropa o monnye*: Small hand drum. For dancing
- *Maracas `dikhwa`*: Seed shakers. Women’s rhythm

*Shallow Basket* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Seroto* - plural *diroto*_  
_Other names: *Nkgo* if used for winnowing, *Seroto sa go fisa* if for grain_  
_In history: "The hands of the home"_

For people interested in culture and history, *the shallow basket `seroto` is one of the oldest and most important tools in every Motswana home.* Before plastic bowls, before shops, the seroto did everything.

*1. WHAT IS A SEROTO?*
*Seroto* = Shallow, flat basket. Like a big plate made of grass.

*THE BASKET*
*Made by*: Women. Weaving is passed mother → daughter  
*Materials*:
- *Grass*: `Mokgwa`, `lehlaka`, `phane` palm. Cut in dry season and dyed
- *Dye*: Natural colors from tree bark and roots. Black, red, brown
- *Pattern*: Tight coil or weave so it doesn’t leak

*Size*: 30cm - 80cm wide. Only 5cm - 10cm deep  
*Shape*: Flat bottom, slightly raised edges

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, seroto shows how Batswana lived with the land and with each other:

*A. FOOD + SURVIVAL*
This was the kitchen tool.  
*Uses:*
1.  *Go fisa mabele* - Winnow grain: Toss sorghum `mabele` in the air. Wind blows chaff away
2.  *Go tlhaba dijo* - Serve food: Pap `bogobe`, meat, wild fruits `marula, morula`
3.  *Go omisa* - Dry food: Spread wild vegetables `morogo`, fish, meat in the sun
4.  *Go hlakola* - Sort: Separate beans, peanuts, pumpkin seeds

Proverb: _"Mosadi yo o sa itseng go loga seroto ga a siame"_ - "A woman who can’t weave a seroto is not ready" = skill + independence

*B. WOMEN'S KNOWLEDGE + ART*
Weaving a seroto takes 3-5 days. 
- *Patterns*: Each tribe has patterns. Bakalanga, Bakwena, Bangwato all different
- *Math*: You must count coils perfectly or it warps
- *Trade*: Women traded seroto for grain, pots, or beads

A good weaver’s seroto could last 10+ years.

*C. COMMUNITY*
- *Work groups*: Women sat in circles weaving and gossiping
- *Gifts*: Bride gave seroto to in-laws as a sign of respect
- *Ceremony*: Used to carry offerings at `pula` rain ceremony

*3. HOW IT’S MADE - THE CRAFT*
1.  *Collect grass*: In winter `mariha` when grass is dry
2.  *Dye*: Boil with `mokoba` bark for brown, `moretlwa` for red
3.  *Start center*: Coil a tight circle
4.  *Build out*: Sew in a spiral with thin grass strips
5.  *Finish edge*: Bind with strong pattern so it doesn’t unravel

Takes skill. One mistake and you start over.

*4. SEROTO TODAY*
Still in every home in villages. Now also art.

*2 uses now:*
1.  *Home use*: Winnowing, serving, drying in rural areas
2.  *Art + Tourism*: Beautiful patterned seroto sold at *Orapa Craft*, *Mowana Craft*, *Maun Craft*. Tourists buy them

*Modern twist*: Some weavers now use bright colors and make them as wall art.

*Law*: Don’t use plastic to copy seroto for ceremonies. Elders prefer traditional grass.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Seroto is the shallow basket of Botswana. 

It’s flat, woven from grass, about the size of a car tire.

For 500 years women used it for everything in the kitchen. Winnow sorghum, serve pap, dry morogo, sort beans.

It’s not just a basket. It’s a skill. Girls learned to weave from age 8. The patterns tell you which tribe made it.

Today you still find women winnowing with seroto in villages. And in Gaborone shops you’ll see them as art. Same grass, same skill."

*Quick facts:*
- *Material*: Phane palm, mokgwa grass
- *Made by*: Women
- *Main uses*: Winnow `go fisa`, serve `go tlhaba`, dry `go omisa`
- *Lasts*: 5-10 years if kept dry
- *Saying*: _"Seroto se tletse"_ - "The basket is full" = we have enough

*See + Buy it:*
- *Orapa House Craft, Gaborone*: Best quality seroto
- *Mowana Craft, Gaborone*: Different tribal patterns
- *Maun Craft, Maun*: Okavango weavers
- *Villages*: Kanye, Mochudi. Watch women weave under trees

*Comparison:*
- *Seroto*: Shallow, flat. For food and winnowing
- *Nkgo*: Deeper basket. For carrying water or grain
- *Lekgetlo*: Fish scoop basket. On a stick

*Ditlana* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Ditlana* - singular *Setlana*_  
_English: Small baskets / Little trays_  
_In history: "The children’s tools and women’s helpers"_

For people interested in culture and history, *ditlana are the small shallow baskets in every Motswana home.* If *seroto* is the big kitchen basket, *ditlana* are its little sisters. Small, but used every single day.

*1. WHAT ARE DITLANA?*
*Ditlana* = Mini versions of `seroto`. Shallow, round, 10cm - 25cm wide.

*THE BASKET*
*Made by*: Women and girls. First weaving project for daughters  
*Materials*:
- *Grass*: `Phane` palm, `mokgwa`, `lehlaka`. Same as seroto
- *Dye*: Natural colors from `moretlwa`, `mokoba` bark
- *Pattern*: Simple. Tight coil so it holds small things

*Shape*: Flat, shallow dish. Like a saucer  
*Difference from seroto*: Seroto is for the family. Ditlana are for individuals or small jobs.

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, ditlana show 3 things about daily Batswana life:

*A. TEACHING GIRLS*
This is where weaving starts.  
- *Age 6-8*: Girl gets grass and is taught to make her first `setlana`
- *Purpose*: Learn counting, patterns, patience
- *Test*: If you can make 3 ditlana that don’t leak, you’re ready to learn `seroto`

Proverb: _"Go simolola ka setlana"_ - "Start with a small basket" = Master small things first

*B. DAILY USE - "THE SIDE DISHES OF LIFE"*
Every woman had 4-5 ditlana. Used for:
1.  *Go tlhaba leswai* - Serve salt
2.  *Go tlhaba dinoko* - Hold spices, pepper
3.  *Go tlhaba morogo* - Side dish of cooked greens
4.  *Go tlhaba dikotwana* - Snacks: peanuts, roasted maize
5.  *Go tlhaba ngwana* - Feed baby: Pap `bogobe` in a small portion

*C. GIFTING + ECONOMY*
- *Dowry*: Bride would bring ditlana for her new home
- *Trade*: Women sold 10 ditlana for grain or beads at the market
- *Hospitality*: Guest was given water and food on a clean setlana

*3. HOW THEY’RE MADE - THE CRAFT*
Same method as seroto, but faster. 2-3 hours per one.

1.  *Start small*: Center coil the size of a coin
2.  *Weave out*: 5-6 rounds only
3.  *Simple edge*: No fancy patterns. Just strong and neat

Because they’re small, girls could practice without wasting grass.

*4. DITLANA TODAY*
Still used in 90% of rural homes. Now also sold as crafts.

*2 uses now:*
1.  *Home*: Every meal has 2-3 ditlana on the mat `lephata` for relish
2.  *Crafts*: Sold in sets at *Mowana Craft, Orapa House*. Tourists love them for holding keys, jewelry

*Modern twist*: Some are now made with bright colors as coasters and trinket trays.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Ditlana are the small baskets of Botswana. 

Think of them as little plates made of grass. About the size of your hand.

Every Motswana woman grew up making them. It’s how girls learned to weave. 

In the home they held salt, morogo, peanuts. One for each person at the meal.

Big basket `seroto` feeds the family. Small basket `setlana` feeds the person.

Today you’ll still see them on every table in the village. And in town they’re sold as crafts."

*Quick facts:*
- *Size*: 10cm - 25cm wide
- *Made by*: Women and girls
- *Main uses*: Salt, relish, snacks, baby food
- *Skill*: First weaving lesson for girls
- *Saying*: _"Setlana se senyenyane se tshwara tse dintsi"_ - "The small basket holds many things" = small things matter

*See + Buy them:*
- *Mowana Craft, Gaborone*: Sets of 6 ditlana in different colors
- *Orapa House, Gaborone*: Traditional plain ones
- *Village markets*: Kanye, Molepolole. Women sell them on mats

*Comparison:*
- *Setlana*: Small. Individual use. 10-25cm
- *Seroto*: Medium-big. Family use. 30-80cm. For winnowing
- *Nkgo*: Deep. For carrying grain or water

*Dietshila tsa Letlalo / Setswana Leather Slippers* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Dietshila* - singular *Setshila*  
_Other names: *Dithako tsa letlalo*, *Dithako tsa setswana*_  
_English: Traditional leather sandals / slippers_  
_In history: "Shoes made from the kraal"_

For people interested in culture and history, *dietshila tsa letlalo were the first shoes of Batswana.* Before factories, before imported shoes, every man, woman and child wore leather from their own cattle. Simple, tough, and made for walking on Kalahari sand.

*1. WHAT ARE THEY?*
*Dietshila tsa letlalo* = Leather slippers/sandals made by hand

*THE SHOE*
*Made by*: Men. Specifically leather workers `badiri ba matlalo`. Women sometimes helped cut.  
*Materials - 100% from cattle:*
1.  *Sole*: Thick cow hide `letlalo la kgomo`. From old oxen. 1cm thick
2.  *Straps*: Thinner leather strips `megala` from calf skin
3.  *Thread*: Animal sinew `methapo` or thin leather strips
4.  *Dye*: Red ochre `letsoku` for color. Black from boiled bark

*Design*: 2 types
- *La go rwala* - Slip-on: One strap across the foot. Like modern sandals
- *La go tlamela* - Tied: 2-3 straps that tie around ankle. For long walks

*Color*: Natural brown, black, or red with `letsoku`

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, dietshila show 3 big Batswana values:

*A. NOTHING WASTED*
When a cow was slaughtered, everything was used.
- *Meat* = food
- *Hide* = dietshila, bags, shields `dithaba`, rope `mogoga`
- *Horns* = spoons, snuff containers

Proverb: _"Kgomo e ja ka dikarolo tsotlhe"_ - "The cow is eaten in all its parts"

*B. WALKING + TRAVEL*
Batswana walked everywhere. 10km - 20km to lands `masimo`, to kgotla, to next village.  
Leather soles were perfect for:
1.  *Sand*: Didn’t sink like bare feet
2.  *Thorns*: Protected from `mathlabane`
3.  *Heat*: Leather breathes. Cooler than plastic

Herders `badisa` wore the toughest ones.

*C. STATUS + AGE*
- *Young men*: Plain brown. For work
- *Elders*: Black or red `letsoku`. Polished. Worn to kgotla
- *Chief*: Finest leather, sometimes with beadwork on straps
- *Women*: Smaller, sometimes with simple patterns cut into leather

First pair was made for a boy at `bogwera` initiation.

*3. HOW THEY’RE MADE - THE CRAFT*
Takes 2-3 days. 

1.  *Cure hide*: Soak, scrape, dry in sun for 1 week
2.  *Cut sole*: Trace foot on thick hide. Cut with sharp knife
3.  *Punch holes*: For straps using hot nail
4.  *Cut straps*: From soft calf skin
5.  *Assemble*: Thread straps through. Sew with sinew
6.  *Harden*: Rub with fat and smoke to make waterproof
7.  *Color*: Rub `letsoku` for red, or charred wood for black

A good pair lasted 2-3 years.

*4. DIETSHILA TODAY*
Plastic shoes are common now, but traditional ones are back.

*Where you still find them:*
1.  *Ceremony*: Weddings, `bogwera`, `bojale`, cultural dances. Men wear them with `leteisi`
2.  *Elders*: Many old men in villages still prefer them
3.  *Craft markets*: *Orapa House Gaborone*, *Mowana Craft*. Tourists buy them
4.  *Tourism*: Cultural villages demonstrate making them

*Modern version*: Now some use rubber tires for soles + leather straps. Lasts longer on tar roads.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Dietshila tsa letlalo are the traditional leather sandals of Botswana. 

They’re made from one cow hide. Thick sole from ox skin, straps from calf.

For 300 years Batswana walked in these. To the lands, to kgotla, to war. They protect from thorns and sand.

Nothing was wasted. When we slaughtered a cow, the skin became shoes.

Today we wear them for culture. At weddings, at dance, in kgotla. Elders still say leather shoes make a man walk straight."

*Quick facts:*
- *Material*: Cow hide, calf straps, sinew
- *Made by*: Men / leather workers
- *Use*: Walking, ceremony, status
- *Care*: Oil with fat so they don’t crack
- *Saying*: _"O na le dieta tse thata"* - "He has tough shoes" = he’s experienced/has walked far

*See + Buy them:*
- *Orapa House, Gaborone*: Best quality handmade dietshila
- *Mowana Craft, Gaborone*: Various sizes
- *Village leather workers*: Kanye, Molepolole, Serowe. Ask for "modiri wa matlalo"
- *Museums*: National Museum Gaborone has 1800s pairs

*Comparison:*
- *Dietshila tsa letlalo*: Leather sandals. Traditional, handmade
- *Dithako*: General word for shoes
- *Dikgama*: Beaded shoes. More for women/ceremony

*Setulo sa Letlalo la Nku / Leopard Skin Chair* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Setulo sa letlalo la nku* or *Setulo sa bogosi*_  
_English: Leopard skin chair / Chief's chair_  
_In history: "The seat of power and ancestors"_

For people interested in culture and history, *a chair covered in leopard skin in Botswana is not furniture. It’s a symbol.* It says who has authority, who speaks for the ancestors, and who protects the people.

*1. WHAT IS IT?*
*Setulo sa nku* = A wooden chair, stool, or skin rug covered with leopard skin `letlalo la nku`

*THE CHAIR*
*Made by*: Royal craftsmen `badiri ba bogosi`. Only for chiefs and elders  
*Parts:*
1.  *Frame*: Carved hardwood `motsentsetla` or `mokoba`. Sometimes 3-legged stool `sedilo`
2.  *Cover*: Whole leopard skin, or patches sewn together. Spots must show
3.  *Details*: Sometimes with cow tail `moswe` or beadwork

*2 main types:*
1.  *Setulo sa bogosi* - Chief's chair: High back, for the kgosi in kgotla
2.  *Letlalo la go dula* - Skin rug: Leopard skin spread on ground for elders to sit on

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, leopard skin = 3 things:

*A. POWER + AUTHORITY*
Only the *kgosi* chief and senior headmen could sit on leopard skin.  
*Why leopard `nku`?*
1.  *Strength*: Leopard is strong but wise. Doesn’t hunt in groups
2.  *Stealth*: Sees everything, heard by no one = good leader
3.  *Connection to ancestors*: Believed leopards carry messages between worlds

Proverb: _"Yo o dulang mo nkung o bua ka lentswe le tona"_ - "He who sits on leopard skin speaks with a loud voice"

In kgotla, when the chief stood up from his leopard skin, everyone stood too.

*B. SPIRITUAL + RITUAL*
- *Rain-making*: Chief sits on leopard skin when praying for `pula`
- *Judgment*: Serious cases were heard with chief on leopard skin
- *Initiation*: `Bogwera` and `bojale` leaders wore leopard skin
- *Burial*: Some chiefs were buried with leopard skin

It was believed the skin protected the leader from evil.

*C. WAR + BRAVERY*
Warriors who killed a leopard alone earned the right to wear its skin.  
It was the highest honor. Meant: "This man is brave and blessed."

*3. LAW TODAY - VERY IMPORTANT*
*Since 1973, hunting leopards is illegal in Botswana.* Leopard is protected under CITES and Wildlife Act.

*What this means:*
1.  *No new skins*: You cannot legally kill a leopard for its skin
2.  *Old skins*: Chiefs may still use old family heirloom skins passed down
3.  *Fines/Jail*: Possessing new illegal leopard skin = serious crime

*Because of this, most "leopard skin chairs" today use:*
- *Faux fur*: Printed fabric that looks like leopard
- *Other skins*: Cow hide with leopard pattern painted
- *Museum pieces*: Old chairs in museums

*4. SETULO SA NKU TODAY*
You won’t see new real leopard skin, but you’ll see the symbol everywhere:

1.  *Kgotla*: Chief’s chair still draped with faux leopard skin
2.  *Cultural events*: Dancers wear faux leopard skin cloths
3.  *Tourism*: Craft shops sell "leopard print" chairs for decor
4.  *Museums*: *National Museum Gaborone*, *Khama III Museum Serowe* have real historical ones

*Modern version*: Designers now make chairs with leopard-print fabric to honor tradition without harming animals.

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"In Setswana culture, the leopard skin chair `setulo sa nku` is the chair of the chief.

Leopards are strong, smart, and solitary. So only the kgosi could sit on leopard skin. It showed he had power and the ancestors’ blessing.

The skin was also used in rain prayers and serious kgotla cases.

Today real leopard hunting is banned to protect them. So we use fake leopard print to keep the meaning. The chair still says 'this is a place of respect and leadership.'"

*Quick facts:*
- *Symbol of*: Chief, elder, judge, spiritual leader
- *Animal*: Leopard `nku` - protected species
- *Legal*: Only antique/heirloom real skins allowed
- *Modern*: Faux leopard print used for culture
- *Saying*: _"O batla go nna mo nkung"_ - "You want to sit on leopard skin" = you want power

*See it:*
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: Historical chief’s chair with leopard skin
- *Khama III Museum, Serowe*: Bangwato royal items
- *Kgotla meetings*: Watch chief’s seat - now usually faux skin
- *Craft shops*: Gaborone - leopard-print chairs for home decor

*Important note:*
If someone offers to sell you "real new leopard skin" in Botswana, it is *illegal*. Report to Department of Wildlife. Real cultural pieces are now made with respect for conservation.

*Comparison:*
- *Setulo sa nku*: Leopard. Highest rank. Chief
- *Setulo sa phofu*: Eland skin. Senior headman
- *Setulo sa kgomo*: Cow hide. Regular elder

*Dishukeka Wig* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Dishukeka* or *Setuku sa dishukeka*_  
_English: Traditional Setswana wig / braided wig_  
_In history: "The crown that shows who you are"_  

Yep you mean *Dishukeka* 👆 Sometimes spelled _dishukeka, tshukudu, tshukeka_. 

For people interested in culture and history, *dishukeka is the traditional hairstyle/wig of Setswana women.* Before extensions and weaves, women spent days making hair that could last months and told your whole story.

### *1. WHAT IS DISHUKEKA?*
*Dishukeka* = A fitted cap/wig made from real human hair + black thread + ochre

#### *THE WIG/HAIRSTYLE*
*Made by*: Older women `bommagogo`. Takes 3-7 days  
*Materials*:
1.  *Base*: Woman’s own hair, braided tight to the scalp
2.  *Thread*: Black cotton or sinew `mogoga`
3.  *Color*: Red ochre `letsoku` mixed with fat `sekgala`
4.  *Add-ons*: Extra human hair, goat hair, or `bogolo` fibers for volume

*Shape*: 3 main styles
1.  *Dishukeka sa bogadi* - Married woman: 2 big horns at front, or 1 big bun at back
2.  *Dishukeka sa ngwana* - Young girl: Many small braids, no ochre yet
3.  *Dishukeka sa mosadimogolo* - Elder: Flat, neat, covered with `letsoku`

*Look*: Dark red-brown from `letsoku`, shiny from fat. Very neat and sculpted.

### *2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, dishukeka was like an ID card:

#### *A. STATUS + AGE*
You could tell everything about a woman just by looking:
1.  *Unmarried girl*: Short braids, no `letsoku`
2.  *Newly married*: Big dishukeka with 2 "horns" `dithaka`. Means "I have a husband"
3.  *Mother*: Bigger, with `letsoku` to show maturity
4.  *Widow/elder*: Dishukeka covered with black cloth `tuku`

Proverb: _"Hlogo ke segolwane sa mosadi"_ - "The head is the crown of a woman"

#### *B. PROTECTION + BEAUTY*
-  *`Letsoku` + fat*: Protected hair from sun and dryness. Made it last 3-6 months
-  *No daily combing*: Once done, you just oil it
-  *Beauty*: Red color was considered beautiful and fertile

#### *C. CEREMONY*
Worn for:
-  *Weddings*: Bride’s dishukeka was the biggest
-  *`Bogadi`*: Showed she was now a wife
-  *`Bojale`*: Girls got first dishukeka after initiation
-  *Kgotla*: Elder women wore neat dishukeka to show respect

### *3. HOW IT’S MADE - THE CRAFT*
1.  *Wash + dry*: Hair must be very clean
2.  *Braid base*: Cornrows tight to scalp
3.  *Build shape*: Sew in extra hair with thread to make horns/bun
4.  *Apply mixture*: `Letsoku` + fat + herbs. Rub in
5.  *Smoke*: Sometimes smoked with `mokolwane` leaves for shine and smell

Lasts 3-6 months. Then you redo it.

### *4. DISHUKEKA TODAY*
Real dishukeka is rare now, but the style is back.

*Where you see it:*
1.  *Cultural dance*: Setapa, borankana dancers wear dishukeka wigs
2.  *Weddings*: Brides wear modern version with extensions
3.  *Crafts*: Wig makers in *Gaborone, Kanye, Mochudi* make "dishukeka wigs" for rent
4.  *Museums*: *National Museum Gaborone* has old dishukeka on display

*Modern version*: Synthetic wig shaped like dishukeka. No `letsoku`, just red color. Worn for 1 day events.

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Dishukeka is the traditional Setswana wig for women. 

It’s not just hair. It’s a message. 

2 horns = married woman. Red color from `letsoku` = respect and beauty. Big and neat = elder.

Women would sit for a week to make it, and it would last for months. The red ochre protected the hair.

Today we don’t do the full `letsoku` process daily, but dancers and brides still wear dishukeka wigs for culture."

#### *Quick facts:*
- *Material*: Hair + thread + `letsoku` + fat
- *Worn by*: Women. Age and marriage status showed in style
- *Lasts*: 3-6 months
- *Color*: Red-brown
- *Saying*: _"O dirile dishukeka"_ - "She has done dishukeka" = she is ready for marriage

#### *See + Get one:*
- *Cultural costume rentals, Gaborone*: For weddings/photos
- *Orapa House Craft*: Sometimes sells display dishukeka
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: See historical ones
- *Cultural festivals*: Maitisong, Dithubaruba - dancers wear them

#### *Comparison:*
- *Dishukeka*: Married woman. Horns/bun. Red
- *Dithapo*: Many thin braids. Young girl
- *Tuku*: Headwrap. Worn over dishukeka by elders

*Made by*: Men. Hunters and leather workers `badiri ba matlalo`

3.  *Cut + Sew*: Shape into tube. Sew with animal sinew `methapo`
4.  *Add strap*: Braided leather for shoulder
5.  *Decorate*: Beads, dye, or leave fur on

Takes 2-3 days. Lasts many years.

Hunting with bows is mostly gone, but the quiver lives on.

5.  *Healers*: Some `dingaka` still use small quiver-style bags for herbs

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"The quiver `mokotla wa metshwana` is the traditional arrow bag of Botswana.

- *Used by*: Hunters and healers
- *Carried*: Over shoulder, arrows pointing up

*Nkgo ya Kgoga / Tortoise Shell Container* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Nkgo ya kgoga* or *Leselo la kgoga*_  
_English: Tortoise shell container / snuff box_  
_In history: "The elder’s purse"_  

For people interested in culture and history, *the tortoise shell in Botswana was the original "plastic container".* Tough, light, and it never leaked. Elders carried it everywhere.

*1. WHAT IS IT?*
*Nkgo ya kgoga* = Empty tortoise shell used as a container

*THE CONTAINER*
*Made by*: Elders and hunters. Any man who found a dead tortoise `kgoga`  
*Animal*: *Leopard tortoise* `kgoga e kgolo` and *hinged tortoise* `kgoga e nnye`  
*Parts used*:
1.  *Top shell* `karapace`: Cut open = bowl or lid
2.  *Bottom shell* `plastron`: Flat = tray or snuff box
3.  *Whole shell*: Hollowed out = bottle or water container

*How they prepared it*:
1.  *Find*: Only use tortoise that died naturally. Killing for shell was taboo
2.  *Clean*: Boil out the inside
3.  *Smoke*: Over fire so it doesn’t smell and won’t crack
4.  *Seal*: Edges rubbed with fat `sekgala`

*Size*: Fits in palm. 8cm - 20cm wide

*2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, kgoga shell had 4 uses:

*A. SNUFF BOX `LEKOPE`*
This is the most famous use.
- *What inside*: `Lekope` tobacco snuff + herbs
- *Who*: Men and women elders. Especially in kgotla
- *How*: Open shell, pinch snuff, share with others. Offering snuff = respect
- *Why tortoise*: Shell keeps snuff dry. Doesn’t break if dropped

Proverb: _"A re abelaneng lekope"_ - "Let’s share snuff" = let’s talk peace

*B. MEDICINE CONTAINER `NKGO YA DITLHARE`*
- *Healers* `dingaka`: Kept powdered herbs, roots, and muti inside
- *Air tight*: Kept medicine dry and strong
- *Small*: Easy to carry to patients

*C. CHILDREN’S TOYS + UTENSILS*
- *Mini bowls*: Kids ate `bogobe` from small shell halves
- *Rattles*: Shell with seeds inside = baby rattle
- *Game pieces*: Used in `diketo` and `morabaraba`

*D. SYMBOL + PROTECTION*
Tortoise `kgoga` means:
1.  *Long life*: Tortoise lives 100+ years
2.  *Patience*: "Go tsamaya ka kgoga" = go slow and steady
3.  *Protection*: Shell = armor. Carrying it wards off bad luck

Elders believed if you carry kgoga shell, you will live long.

*3. LAW TODAY - VERY IMPORTANT*
*All tortoises in Botswana are protected* under Wildlife Act and CITES.

*What this means:*
1.  *No collecting*: You cannot pick up or kill tortoises for shells
2.  *Old shells only*: Family heirloom shells from before 1990 are allowed
3.  *Fine*: Possessing new wild tortoise shell = illegal

*Because of this, today we use:*
- *Gourds* `magapu` cut like shells
- *Wooden snuff boxes* carved to look like tortoise
- *Museum pieces* for display

*4. NKGO YA KGOGA TODAY*
You won’t find new ones, but the symbol is still strong.

*Where you see it:*
1.  *Kgotla*: Elder men still carry wooden "tortoise" snuff boxes
2.  *Museums*: *National Museum Gaborone* has 1800s snuff shells
3.  *Crafts*: Carvers make wooden tortoise containers for tourists
4.  *Sayings*: _"O na le pelo ya kgoga"_ - "He has a tortoise heart" = he is patient

*5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Nkgo ya kgoga is the tortoise shell container of Botswana. 

Our grandfathers used it as a snuff box and medicine bottle. 

Tortoise means long life and patience. The shell is hard so snuff stays dry even in the Kalahari.

You would never kill a tortoise for it. You only used one that died naturally.

*Quick facts:*

- *Used for*: Snuff, medicine, bowls, toys
- *Symbol*: Long life, patience, respect
- *Legal*: Only antique/heirloom shells allowed
- *Saying*: _"O tsamaya ka kgoga"_ - "He walks like a tortoise" = he is patient and wise

*See it:*
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: Snuff boxes and medicine containers
- *Phuthadikobo Museum, Mochudi*: Bakwena elder’s items
- *Craft markets*: Wooden tortoise snuff boxes

*Comparison:*
- *Nkgo ya kgoga*: Tortoise shell. For snuff/medicine

*Otjihandal le Kakonzo* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana + Herero influence_  
_English: The grinding stone and the neck rattle_  
_In history: "Sound and work of women"_  

You’re talking about 2 things that are always mentioned together in traditional Botswana/Namibia border cultures, especially *Herero, Hambukushu and Bakgalagadi*. 

### *1. OTJIHANDAL = THE GRINDING STONE*  
_Setswana: *Loora* or *Tshilo*_  
_Herero: *Otjihanda*_  

#### *WHAT IS IT?*
*Otjihandal* = Big flat grinding stone + smaller hand stone for grinding grain

*Parts:*
1.  *Lejwe le legolo* - Base stone: Flat, granite. Sits on ground
2.  *Lejwe le lennye* - Hand stone: Round stone you hold

*Used for:*
- *Go sila mabele* - Grinding sorghum/millet into flour `bupi`
- *Go sila dinawa* - Grinding beans for `dikgobe`
- *Go sila ditlhare* - Grinding herbs for medicine

*Sound*: `Shu-shu-shu` - women grind early morning. That sound means "home is awake"

*Cultural meaning*: 
- *Women’s work*: Passing skill from mother to daughter
- *Patience*: _"O sila ka otjihandal"_ = you are hardworking
- *Community*: Women grind together and sing

### *2. KAKONZO = THE NECK RATTLE / NECKLACE*  
_Setswana: *Konkoni* or *Seopedi*_  
_Herero: *Okaronzo*_  

#### *WHAT IS IT?*
*Kakonzo* = Necklace with seeds, beads, or metal pieces that make sound

*Made from:*
1.  *Dipeo* - Seeds: `morama` seeds, `morama` pods
2.  *Dithaka* - Beads: Glass, bone
3.  *Tshipi* - Metal: Bottle caps, copper pieces
4.  *String*: Leather `mogoga` or cotton

*Worn by*: Women and girls, especially dancers  
*Sound*: `Tsa-tsa-tsa` when walking or dancing

*Cultural meaning:*
1.  *Dance*: Makes rhythm for `borankana`, `setapa`. No drums needed
2.  *Beauty*: Shows you are a woman. More sound = more confident
3.  *Status*: Married women wear heavier kakonzo
4.  *Protection*: Seeds believed to ward off bad spirits

Proverb: _"Mosadi o utlwala ka kakonzo"_ - "You hear a woman by her rattle"

### *3. WHY THEY’RE MENTIONED TOGETHER: "OTJIHANDAL LE KAKONZO"*
In Herero and Bakgalagadi culture this phrase means *"A complete woman"*

*Otjihandal* = Work. She feeds the home. Responsible.  
*Kakonzo* = Joy. She dances. She is beautiful. She celebrates.

Together they mean: _A woman who works hard AND knows how to live and celebrate._

At weddings and `bojale`, elders will say: _"A o na le otjihandal le kakonzo?"_  
="Do you have the grinding stone and the rattle?" = "Are you ready to be a wife?"

### *4. TODAY*
*Otjihandal*: Mostly replaced by mills. But still used in villages and for traditional beer `bojalwa`.  
*Kakonzo*: Still worn by dancers. Sold at *Orapa House*, *Mowana Craft*. Modern ones use plastic beads but keep the sound.

You’ll hear both at cultural festivals: Women grinding while wearing kakonzo. `Shu-shu` + `tsa-tsa`

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT:*
"Otjihandal le kakonzo are 2 things that define a Setswana/Herero woman.

*Otjihandal* is the grinding stone. It’s work, food, and providing.  
*Kakonzo* is the neck rattle. It’s dance, beauty, and celebration.

Elders say a woman needs both. You must work, but you must also bring joy. 
That’s why at weddings we give girls both: a grinding stone and beads."

- *Kakonzo*: Neck rattle. Symbol of beauty + dance

#### *WHAT IS IT?*
*German print* = Thick cotton fabric with big floral patterns. Bright colors: blue, red, green, yellow

*Why "German"?*  
In the 1890s German missionaries and traders brought this fabric to Namibia. Herero women loved it and made it their own. Even after Germans left, the print stayed.

*How it’s worn:*
1.  *Ohorokova* - The big dress: 3-4 layers of petticoats `diese` to make it stand out
2.  *Shawl/Kobo*: Wrapped over shoulders. For warmth + beauty
3.  *Appron*: Sometimes worn over dress

*Colors mean things:*
- *Red*: Married women, strength
- *Blue*: Respect, calm
- *Green*: Growth, fertility
- *Multi-color*: Celebration

### *2. DOUKE / DOEK*  
_Setswana: *Tuku* / *Doeke*_  
_Herero: *Otjikatji*_  
_English: Headwrap / horned headscarf_  

#### *WHAT IS IT?*
*Douke* = The famous horned headwrap. Made from the same German print fabric

*How it’s made:*
1.  *Take 1 big square* of German print, ∼1.5m x 1.5m
2.  *Fold + tie* to make 2 big "horns" or "cow ears" at the front
3.  *Shape*: Looks like cow horns `dithaka tsa kgomo`

*Why horns?*  
Cattle `dikgomo` are everything to Herero people. The douke honors the cow.  
Proverb: _"Hlogo ya mosadi e tshwana le kgomo"_ - "A woman’s head is like a cow"

*Who wears it:*
- *Married women*: Must wear douke. Shows "I am taken"
- *Elders*: Bigger, taller horns = more respect
- *Young girls*: Smaller douke, or just headscarf

### *3. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, this outfit tells the Herero story:

#### *A. RESISTANCE + PRIDE*
After the 1904 war, Herero women took the German fabric and made it _theirs_.  
Instead of European hats, they made *otjikatji* horns.  
Today it says: _"We survived. We are still Herero."_

#### *B. STATUS + IDENTITY*
You can tell everything from the outfit:
1.  *Married*: Full dress + big douke + many petticoats
2.  *Widow*: Black or dark blue German print + covered douke
3.  *Girl*: No douke yet, just scarf
4.  *Church*: Best German print for Sunday

#### *C. BOTSWANA CONNECTION*
Herero people crossed into Botswana during the war. Today in *Ghanzi, Dkar, Charles Hill* you’ll see this outfit at:
- *Weddings*
- *Herero Day* - August
- *Church* 
- *Cultural dance*

### *4. HOW IT’S MADE TODAY*
1.  *Buy fabric*: 6-8 meters of German print for 1 dress. *Orapa House*, *African Fabric shops in Gaborone* sell it
2.  *Sew*: Tailor makes the big layered dress
3.  *Tie douke*: Elder women teach young girls. Takes 10 minutes
4.  *Accessories*: Add copper bangles `mekgele`, beads `dithapo`

Cost: P800 - P2500 for full set depending on fabric

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT:*
"German print shawl le douke is the traditional outfit of Herero women in Botswana and Namibia.

*German print* is the bright floral fabric. Germans brought it, but Herero women made it famous.  
*Douke* is the headwrap with 2 horns. It looks like cow horns because cattle are sacred.

When a woman wears this, she is saying: I am Herero, I am married, I am proud. 
The bigger the douke, the more respect she gets.

You’ll see it every Sunday in church and at weddings in Ghanzi."

#### *Quick facts:*
- *Fabric*: German print cotton. Big flowers
- *Douke*: Horned headwrap. Symbol of cattle + marriage
- *Worn by*: Herero, Hambukushu, Bakgalagadi women
- *Colors*: Red=married, Blue=respect, Black=widow
- *Saying*: _"O apere ka seriti"_ - "She is dressed with dignity"

#### *See + Buy them:*

*Lekuka la Mashi / Milk Container* 🇧🇼  
_Setswana: *Lekuka* - singular *Sekuka*_  
_Other names: *Nkgo ya mashi*, *Seboko*_  
_English: Milk gourd / Milk container_  
_In history: "The refrigerator of the Kalahari"_  

For people interested in culture and history, *the milk container in Botswana was everything.* Before fridges, before plastic, this gourd kept milk fresh, made sour milk, and carried wealth.

### *1. WHAT IS IT?*
*Lekuka* = Dried gourd used to store, ferment, and carry milk

#### *THE CONTAINER*
*Made by*: Women. From `lephala` or `sephala` gourd plant  
*How it’s made:*
1.  *Grow*: Plant gourd. Let it grow big and round
2.  *Dry*: Leave for 3-6 months until hard and light
3.  *Clean*: Cut top off, scoop seeds out, wash with ash and water
4.  *Smoke*: Smoke inside with `mokolwane` leaves. Makes it smell good and keeps it clean
5.  *Seal*: Plug with grass `lehlaka` or leather stopper

*Size*: Small `sekuka` = 1L for drinking. Big `lekuka` = 10-20L for family  
*Color*: Starts green, turns yellow-brown, then black inside from use. Black = good, means it’s "seasoned"

### *2. THE CULTURE + HISTORY BEHIND IT*
For historians, lekuka had 4 jobs:

#### *A. STORING MILK `GO BEKA MASHI`*
- *Fresh milk* `mashi a masweu`: Poured in morning, drunk same day
- *Sour milk* `madila`: Left 2-3 days to ferment. The gourd makes it taste better
- *Why gourd*: Keeps milk cool in heat. Doesn’t make it taste like plastic

Proverb: _"Lekuka ga le latlhe mashi"_ - "A gourd doesn’t waste milk" = be careful with what you have

#### *B. MAKING MADILA*
This is the big one. 
1.  *Pour fresh milk* in lekuka
2.  *Shake* every day `go tshikinya`
3.  *In 3 days* = thick sour milk `madila`
4.  *Drink* with `bogobe` or on its own

The inside of an old lekuka has good bacteria. New milk sours faster.

#### *C. STATUS + WEALTH*
- *Young girl*: 1 small lekuka
- *Married woman*: 3-5 lekuuka. Shows she has cows
- *Elder*: Biggest, blackest lekuka. Shows experience

Saying: _"O na le mashi le lekuuka"_ - "She has milk and a gourd" = she is a good wife

#### *D. GIFT + CEREMONY*
- *Bohadi*: Groom’s family brings lekuka full of madila
- *Welcoming guests*: Offer madila in a clean lekuka = respect
- *Healing*: `Dingaka` use special lekuuka for medicine milk

### *3. RULES OF LEKUKA - VERY IMPORTANT*
1.  *Never use soap*: Wash with ash and hot water only. Soap spoils milk
2.  *Never put water*: Only milk. Water makes it rot
3.  *Keep in shade*: Sun cracks it
4.  *One lekuka = one purpose*: Milk lekuka never used for beer

Women teach daughters: _"Lekuka ke seriti sa mosadi"_ - "The gourd is a woman’s dignity"

### *4. LEKUKA TODAY*
Plastic and buckets replaced many, but lekuuka is still loved.

*Where you see it:*
1.  *Villages*: Grandmothers still make madila in lekuka. Tastes better
2.  *Cultural tourism*: *Orapa House*, *Mowana Craft* sell decorative lekuuka
3.  *Ceremony*: Weddings and `bojale` still use lekuka for madila
4.  *Museums*: *National Museum Gaborone* has 100-year old lekuuka

*Modern version*: Plastic "lekuka" shaped containers sold for madila

### *5. HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Lekuka is the traditional milk gourd of Botswana.

Women cut and dry a gourd, then use it to store milk and make sour milk `madila`.

The gourd keeps milk cool and makes it taste good. The older and blacker the inside, the better.

A woman with many lekuuka meant her family had cows and she was a good wife. 
Today we still use it for ceremony and because madila from lekuka tastes the best."

#### *Quick facts:*
- *Material*: Dried gourd `lephala`
- *Used by*: Women
- *Used for*: Fresh milk, sour milk `madila`, transport
- *Lasts*: 5-10 years if cared for
- *Sound*: `Shu-shu` when shaking to make madila
- *Saying*: _"Tlhokomela lekuka la gago"_ - "Take care of your gourd" = take care of your home

#### *See + Get one:*
- *Village markets*: Ghanzi, Molepolole. Old women sell them
- *Orapa House, Gaborone*: Decorated lekuuka
- *National Museum, Gaborone*: Historical milk containers
- *Farms*: Ask for `lephala` seeds to grow your own

#### *Care tip:*
To season new lekuka: Put hot water + ash, shake, rinse. Then put milk. First 3 times will taste like gourd, then it’s perfect.

#### *Comparison:*
- *Lekuka*: Gourd. For milk
- *Nkgo ya letsopa*: Clay pot. For water/beer
- *Seroto*: Basket. For grain

*Borankana* 🇧🇼

*1. WHAT IS BORANKANA?*
*Borankana* = Traditional Setswana dance of joy and celebration. 
Danced at weddings, `bogwera/bojale`, `kgotla` meetings, and cultural events.
Men and women dance in lines, stamping feet and clapping. _Very energetic!_

*2. TRADITIONAL WOMEN'S DANCE WEAR FOR BORANKANA*

Women who dance Borankana wear *bright, layered, and noisy outfits* so the dance looks and sounds alive.

*THE MAIN PIECES:*

1.  *LEKGAREBE / SEKGABA - THE SKIRT*  
    _This is the most important piece_  
    - *Material*: Layers of pleated fabric, or traditional `shweshwe` / `tekeri`
    - *Colors*: Bright red, blue, green, yellow. Often 2-3 colors layered
    - *Length*: Long, goes to ankles so you can see it flare when you stamp
    - *Job*: Flies out when women do the stomping `go phatlha ka dinao`

2.  *LETSELA / SEHUBA - THE TOP & WRAP*  
    - *Top*: `Shirt` or `blouse` matching the skirt, often with sleeves
    - *Shawl/Wrap*: `Kobo` or `sehubu` over shoulders. Bright patterned cloth

3.  *LESETSE / DITLHOMAMA - THE BEADS & JEWELRY*  
    - *Necklace* `setswane`: Big beaded necklace
    - *Headband* `kefelwana`: Beaded headband or `tukwi` scarf tied on head
    - *Armbands* `dithomama`: Beads on arms and legs
    - *Earrings*: Big beaded ones

4.  *DISEGA / DITLHANKANA - THE NOISE MAKERS*  
    _This is what makes Borankana sound like Borankana_  
    - *Ditala tsa maoto*: Leg rattles made from goat hooves `dithope tsa podi` or bottle caps tied in a band
    - *Job*: Go `shaka-shaka` when women stamp. Adds rhythm to the drums

5.  *DIETE - SHOES*  
    - *Traditional*: Bare feet so you can feel the ground and stamp harder
    - *Modern*: Simple flat sandals

*3. THE FULL LOOK*
*From head to toe:*  
`Kefelwana` headband → `Setswane` beads → `Shirt` → `Kobo` shawl → `Lekgarebe` layered skirt → `Ditala` leg rattles → Bare feet

*Colors*: Bold. Red is most common because it means celebration and strength.

*4. CULTURAL MEANING*
1.  *Layers = Joy*: The more layers, the more the skirt flies when you dance
2.  *Beads = Culture*: Each pattern can mean age group or village
3.  *Rattles = Music*: Women are part of the band. Their feet are drums
4.  *Bright colors = Celebration*: Borankana is for happy occasions

Proverb: _"Mosadi wa Borankana o a phatsima"_ - "The Borankana woman shines"

*5. MEN vs WOMEN IN BORANKANA*
*Women*: Flowy skirts `lekgarebe`, beads, leg rattles  
*Men*: `Leteisi` pants, shirt, `mokorotlo` hat, and also leg rattles

They face each other and dance together.

*6. WHERE TO SEE + GET THE OUTFIT*
1.  *Cultural events*: Botswana Day, President’s Day, weddings
2.  *Cultural villages*: Ghanzi, Oodi
3.  *Mowana Craft, Gaborone*: Sell `lekgarebe` skirts and `ditala`
4.  *Makers*: Women in villages still sew them. Ask for `mosadi wa go roka diaparo tsa setso`

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT:*
"Women who dance *Borankana* wear a bright layered skirt called *lekgarebe*, a matching top and shawl, lots of beads, and *leg rattles* made from goat hooves.

When they stamp their feet the skirts flare and the rattles go `shaka-shaka`. 
It’s colorful, loud, and full of joy because Borankana is a dance of celebration."

*Quick facts:*
- *Dance*: Borankana
- *Main skirt*: Lekgarebe - layered, pleated
- *Key item*: Ditala tsa maoto - leg rattles
- *Colors*: Red, blue, green, yellow
- *Feet*: Usually bare
- *Meaning*: Joy, community, culture
