*Borra-borre dance* in Botswana 🇧🇼 

First: I think you might mean *"Borankana"* or *"Boranaka"* dance? Because "borra-borre" isn’t a widely documented name in Botswana’s cultural records.  
But there _is_ a dance called *"Borankana"* that comes from the *Bakgalagadi people* in the Kgalagadi/Kalahari region, and it’s often what people are referring to when they ask about this. It also gets confused with *"Botsankana"* or *"Borankana/Boranaka"* in oral history.

Let me break down what’s known for you:

*1. WHERE IT COMES FROM*
*People*: Primarily the *Bakgalagadi* and *Basarwa/San* communities in the Kgalagadi District, southern and western Botswana.  
*Region*: Kalahari Desert areas - places like Ghanzi, Hukuntsi, Tsabong.  
*History*: It’s a very old community dance, pre-colonial. It was done during gatherings, after hunting, during rain ceremonies, weddings, and to welcome visitors.

*2. WHAT THE DANCE LOOKS LIKE*
*The vibe*: Grounded, rhythmic, communal. Not performative for tourists - it’s participatory.  
*Movements*: 
- Men and women dance in a circle or two lines facing each other
- Heavy stomping with feet. The "thud" on the sand is part of the music
- Shoulder shaking + hip movements. Torso stays quite upright
- Clapping and call-and-response singing leads the rhythm
- Sometimes dancers carry sticks or wear rattles `dichaba` / `legora` on ankles made from seed pods so the dance makes its own percussion

*Music*: 
- Vocals are the main instrument. Leader calls, group responds
- Hand clapping, foot stomping, ankle rattles
- Sometimes accompanied by `segaba` - a 1-string violin, or drums

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY*
For the Bakgalagadi, Borankana is about 3 things:

1.  *Community bonding*: Everyone joins. Age doesn’t matter. It breaks down hierarchy. 
2.  *Storytelling*: The songs tell history - migration stories, hunting stories, jokes about daily life, praises of the land.
3.  *Resilience*: In the Kalahari, life is hard. The dance is a way to celebrate survival, rain, and being together despite harsh conditions. The stomping is literally "marking the earth" - we are still here.

It’s not a "performance dance" like you see on stage. Historically, if there was a wedding or a successful hunt, people would just start, and everyone would know what to do.

*4. TODAY*
Borankana is part of Botswana’s efforts to preserve intangible heritage.  
You’ll see it at:
- *President’s Day Celebrations* in Botswana
- *National Cultural Festivals*
- *Tourism cultural villages* in Ghanzi 
Groups like the *Kuru Dance Group* from the Kalahari have performed Borankana internationally to teach about San and Bakgalagadi culture.

*Important note*
Because Botswana has 8 major tribes and many dialects, dance names shift. "Borra-borre" could also be a local pronunciation of Borankana, or a family/clan-specific version. Oral cultures change names as they move. 

If you heard "borra-borre" from someone specific, it may be the name used in one village in Kgalagadi.

---

*How to explain it to people interested in history:*
"Borankana is a Kalahari circle dance from Botswana’s Bakgalagadi people. It’s hundreds of years old. Through stomping, clapping, and call-and-response songs, it records history, celebrates community survival in the desert, and lets everyone participate regardless of age or status. It’s not about watching a star - it’s about everyone becoming the rhythm together."

*Hosanna Chokwe* in Botswana 🇧🇼

This one is a really cool mix of history, religion, and dance — and people interested in culture/history love it because it shows how Botswana blended outside influence with local tradition.

*1. WHERE IT COMES FROM*
*People*: The *Chokwe people* — an ethnic group originally from Angola, DRC, and Zambia. A smaller Chokwe community migrated into *northwestern Botswana*, mainly around *Chobe District, Kasane, and Pandamatenga* area over the last 100-150 years.  
*Name*: "Hosanna" comes from the Christian church hymn "Hosanna" / praise to God. When Christian missionaries arrived in the late 1800s-early 1900s, many Chokwe communities converted but kept their dance and music style.  
So *Hosanna Chokwe = "Praise Dance of the Chokwe"*

It’s not an "ancient pre-colonial" dance. It’s a *20th century cultural fusion* — African Chokwe rhythm + Christian gospel message.

*2. WHAT THE DANCE LOOKS LIKE*
*The vibe*: Joyful, energetic, spiritual. It’s a church dance but also performed at cultural festivals.  
*Movements*:
- *Circle or line formation*. Men and women dance together
- *Fast footwork* with lots of stomping and shuffling. Very rhythmic
- *Arm movements*: Arms raised up "in praise", clapping overhead, sometimes waving handkerchiefs or branches
- *Drumming is central*: Uses traditional Chokwe drums `ngoma` plus hand claps
- *Call and response singing*: Leader sings a gospel line in Chokwe or English, group answers "Hosanna! Hosanna!"

*Clothing*: 
- Women often wear long colorful skirts + head wraps
- Men wear shirts and trousers, sometimes with sashes
- At festivals they add beads and traditional Chokwe patterns

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For the Chokwe community in Botswana, Hosanna serves 3 purposes:

1.  *Faith + Identity*: It let Chokwe Christians keep their drumming and dance style while singing Christian songs. Instead of losing culture to the church, they adapted the church to their culture.
2.  *Community cohesion*: It’s performed at weddings, funerals, harvest thanks-givings, and Sunday service. Everyone knows the steps. It pulls the community together.
3.  *History of migration*: The dance is proof of how Chokwe people moved south and settled in Botswana, bringing their music but adapting to new neighbors — Tswana, Kalanga, Ndebele.

Historically, missionaries first tried to ban "traditional dancing". Chokwe communities responded with: "Okay, we’ll dance for God instead." That’s how Hosanna was born.

*4. TODAY*
You’ll see Hosanna Chokwe at:
- *Church services* in Chobe District 
- *Botswana National Cultural Festivals* and *President’s Day* — Chokwe groups perform it as part of "cultural diversity" showcases
- *Tourism events* in Kasane/Chobe — it’s one of the dances used to represent northwestern Botswana culture

Groups like the *Chobe Chokwe Cultural Group* keep it alive and teach younger people. Because the Chokwe population in Botswana is small, this dance is also a way to make sure their language and songs don’t disappear.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO HISTORY/CULTURE PEOPLE:*
"Hosanna Chokwe is a 20th-century praise dance from the Chokwe community in northern Botswana. It was born when Chokwe migrants blended their traditional drumming and dance with Christian gospel music from missionaries. Through fast footwork, circle dancing, and call-and-response singing of 'Hosanna', it became both a church worship style and a way for the Chokwe to preserve their identity in Botswana. It’s a living example of cultural adaptation — not loss."

*Key difference vs other Botswana dances:*
- *Setapa* = Tswana wedding dance, slow and proud
- *Borankana* = Bakgalagadi/San desert dance, grounded stomping  
- *Hosanna Chokwe* = Chokwe gospel dance, fast, uplifting, arms-up praise

*Phathisi / Phathisis* in Botswana 🇧🇼

Phathisi is one of Botswana’s most iconic traditional dances. If you’re explaining Botswana culture and history to people, this is the one they’ll recognize first — it’s performed at President’s Day, national events, and cultural festivals.

*1. WHERE IT COMES FROM*
*People*: Originated with the *Bangwato* people of the *Central District*, especially around *Serowe*. The Bangwato are part of the larger *Batswana* nation.  
*History*: Pre-colonial roots, but the version we see today was formalized in the 1900s. The name "Phathisi" comes from the Setswana word _go phatha_ = "to clap/hit/strike". So literally "the clapping dance".  
*Purpose originally*: A dance of celebration, courtship, and community pride. It was done after successful harvests, weddings, and to welcome chiefs/visitors.

*2. WHAT THE DANCE LOOKS LIKE*
Phathisi is high-energy and very rhythmic. It’s not subtle.

*Movements*:
- *Circle formation*: Men and women in a big circle, sometimes two circles — men inside, women outside
- *Footwork*: Fast, stomping steps. Dancers hit the ground hard. The dust/sand rising is part of it
- *Clapping*: This is the signature. Sharp, synchronized hand claps on the beat. _Phatha phatha phatha_
- *Shoulder + chest movements*: Lots of shoulder shimmies and chest pops
- *Singing*: A lead singer calls, the group responds in chorus. Songs are about love, cattle, bravery, praising the chief

*Music*: 
- Hand clapping is the main percussion
- Foot stomping 
- Sometimes 1-2 drums or `segaba` 1-string fiddle
- No modern instruments. It’s all body + voice

*Attire*:
- *Women*: `leteisi` patterned dresses, `megopo` pleated skirts, headscarves `tukwi`, beads
- *Men*: `metshego` loincloths or shorts with shirts, sometimes with cowhide rattles on legs `dichaba` that jingle when they stomp

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For historians and culture people, Phathisi matters for 3 reasons:

1.  *Community democracy*: Anyone can join. Age, status doesn’t matter. At a Phathisi, the chief’s son and the farmer’s daughter dance the same steps. It was how Batswana reinforced "botho" — humanity/community.
2.  *Oral history*: The songs are the newspaper. They praise heroes, tease politicians, remember droughts, celebrate weddings. Before newspapers, this is how news traveled.
3.  *Identity marker*: After independence in 1966, Botswana used Phathisi as a "national dance". It’s performed to show Batswana culture to the world because it’s energetic, visual, and clearly "Botswana".

During colonial times, missionaries tried to ban it as "too wild". Batswana kept it alive in villages. After independence it made a big comeback as a symbol of national pride.

*4. TODAY*
You will see Phathisi at:
- *President’s Day Celebrations* every July — it’s always the finale
- *National Cultural Festivals* and school competitions 
- *Tourism* — cultural villages in Gaborone and Maun
- *Weddings* — still done socially in Central District

Groups like *Wimbo Arts* and *National Dance Ensemble* perform stylized stage versions, but in villages it’s still done the old way: circle, dust, clapping till hands hurt.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Phathisi is Botswana’s national circle dance from the Bangwato people. For over 100 years it’s been done with fast stomping and sharp clapping to tell stories, celebrate harvests, and bring the community together. There are no spectators — if you’re there, you dance. The claps are the drum. The feet mark the earth. After independence it became a symbol of Batswana identity because it shows botho: everyone is equal in the circle."

*Quick comparison with other Botswana dances:*
- *Setapa*: Slower, more graceful, bride’s dance
- *Borankana*: Kalahari, grounded, from Bakgalagadi/San 
- *Hosanna Chokwe*: Northern, gospel-influenced, arms-up praise
- *Phathisi*: Central, loud, fast clapping, national symbol

*Setapa Dance* in Botswana 🇧🇼

Setapa is _the_ traditional dance most associated with Batswana weddings and celebrations. If someone asks "what’s a classic Botswana dance?", historians and cultural guides will almost always start with Setapa.

*1. WHERE IT COMES FROM*
*People*: Originated with the *Batlhaping* and *Bahurutshe* people in the *Southern and South-Eastern districts* — areas around Kanye, Lobatse, Moshupa. It later spread and became popular across all Batswana groups.  
*History*: Pre-colonial, but it became the "wedding dance" in the 1800s-1900s. The word _Setapa_ comes from _go tapa_ = "to step rhythmically / to stamp".  
*Purpose originally*: A courtship and wedding dance. It was performed by young women to show grace, discipline, and readiness for marriage. Chiefs would also have Setapa performed to welcome dignitaries.

*2. WHAT THE DANCE LOOKS LIKE*
Setapa is elegant, controlled, and rhythmic. It’s the opposite of wild — it’s about poise.

*Movements*:
- *Lines or semi-circles*: Women in front, men behind or on the sides. It’s very organized
- *Footwork*: Soft, precise stepping and heel-to-toe taps. Not heavy stomping like Phathisi. You "kiss" the ground
- *Hands on hips*: Women keep hands on waist/hips. This shows posture and control
- *Upper body still*: The focus is all on the feet and the timing. Shoulders stay level
- *Singing*: Slow to mid-tempo songs. Lead singer calls, group responds. Songs are about love, respect, marriage, praising the bride’s family

*Music*:
- Hand clapping and thigh-slapping for rhythm
- Foot tapping 
- Sometimes a drum or `segaba` but often it’s just voices + body percussion
- The rhythm is what dancers must "catch" — if you’re off-beat it’s obvious

*Attire*:
- *Women*: `leteisi` or `wax print` long skirts, doek `tukwi` on head, beads, and `makgabe` ankle rattles that make a soft sound with each step
- *Men*: Shirts, trousers, sometimes carry a stick `molamu`. They sing and clap more than dance

*3. WHAT IT MEANS CULTURALLY / HISTORICALLY*
For people studying Botswana history, Setapa tells you 3 things about Batswana culture:

1.  *Values*: It teaches _botho_ and discipline. Young women trained for months to dance Setapa perfectly. Messy dancing = bad manners. Good dancing = you come from a good home.
2.  *Courtship*: Historically, men would watch Setapa to see which young women had grace and patience. The dance was part of marriage negotiations.
3.  *Respect for elders*: The dance is slow and respectful. You don’t show off. You show unity. At weddings today, the bride’s family will perform Setapa to honor the groom’s family.

During missionary and colonial times, Setapa survived because it looked "decent" — no wild movements. So it was accepted in church and school. That’s why it’s still so common today.

*4. TODAY*
You’ll see Setapa at:
- *Weddings* — it’s still THE wedding dance in Botswana. No Setapa = not a proper wedding
- *Cultural festivals, President’s Day, Independence Day*
- *Schools* — taught as part of culture classes
- *Tourism* — performed at cultural villages to show "traditional Botswana grace"

Unlike Phathisi which is high energy for everyone, Setapa is usually performed by women’s groups who have practiced. But at a real wedding, aunties will pull everyone in.

*HOW TO EXPLAIN IT TO PEOPLE INTERESTED IN HISTORY:*
"Setapa is Botswana’s traditional wedding dance from the southern part of the country. It’s a dance of elegance and discipline. Women dance in lines with soft footwork, hands on hips, while singing about love and family. For over 200 years it has been used to celebrate marriage, teach young women posture and respect, and welcome important guests. The beauty of Setapa is that it’s not about individual performance — it’s about moving as one, in time, with dignity."

*Quick comparison with other Botswana dances:*
- *Phathisi*: Loud, fast clapping, everyone joins, circle
- *Borankana*: Kalahari, heavy stomping, grounded 
- *Hosanna Chokwe*: Northern, gospel, arms-up praise
- *Setapa*: Southern, elegant, graceful stepping, wedding-focused
